## 1. The decision at the heart of vulnerability management

Organisations often discover more vulnerabilities than they can remediate immediately. A scan may return dozens or hundreds of findings, but teams have limited time, resources, and maintenance windows. They must decide which issues to address first.

This is **vulnerability triage**. The word comes from medicine: when there are more patients than available clinicians, patients are assessed and the most urgent receive care first. Vulnerability triage follows the same idea—assess which vulnerabilities create the greatest risk and prioritise those, while lower-risk findings wait.

Triage is not patching. Patching is the remediation action; triage is the judgement that determines the order of work. A good order reduces the greatest amount of risk with the resources currently available.

Security analysts and vulnerability management teams perform this task regularly. The goal is not simply to sort findings by severity label; it is to explain a defensible, context-aware fix-first order.

## 2. How to judge real risk

**Severity is the starting point, not the whole answer.** CVSS (Day 78) provides a general severity assessment, but real risk depends on the environment where the vulnerability exists.

### Severity
CVSS severity indicates how dangerous a vulnerability is in general. Critical, high, medium, and low labels provide an initial view. A critical finding may not be the top priority in a particular environment, and a high- or medium-severity finding may become more urgent because of context.

### Exposure / reachability
Ask whether an attacker can actually reach the vulnerable component. An internet-facing system is generally more exposed than an isolated internal system. Exposure influences likelihood: the more reachable the vulnerability, the more urgent it may be.

### Value and impact
Ask what the vulnerable system does and what would be lost if it were compromised. A customer database or business-critical server can have much greater impact than a disposable test machine with no valuable data. This is the impact side of risk (Day 67).

### Active exploitation
Is the vulnerability being exploited in the wild right now? If attackers are actively using it, the threat is immediate and the patch window (Day 79) is already relevant. Active exploitation can make a vulnerability much more urgent.

### Patch availability and effort
Is a fix available and practical to apply? A readily available patch for a high-risk issue may be a straightforward priority. If a critical vulnerability cannot be patched, the organisation may need compensating controls (Day 77).

### Putting the factors together
Real risk is severity considered alongside exposure, system value/impact, active exploitation, and patch availability. A severe, exposed, actively exploited vulnerability on a valuable system with a ready patch is a strong fix-first candidate. A moderate vulnerability on an isolated, low-value system with no exploitation evidence and difficult remediation may be able to wait.

## 3. Example: turning scan findings into a triage order

The lesson gives a four-finding scenario. The final order differs from a ranking based only on severity.

### Finding A — Critical, but not applicable
A critical vulnerability is reported in software the organisation does not actually run. Because the affected software is absent, there is nothing in the environment to exploit. This is not a meaningful risk for the stated environment and should be removed from the applicable triage list or placed at the bottom pending validation.

**Reasoning:** Severity alone does not establish risk. Confirming applicability matters.

### Finding B — High on a public web server, actively exploited
A high-severity vulnerability affects an internet-facing web server. A patch is available, and the vulnerability is reported as actively exploited in the wild.

**Reasoning:** This is the clearest top priority: high severity, maximum exposure, active exploitation, and a practical fix.

### Finding C — Critical on an internal customer database
A critical vulnerability affects an internal database server containing customer data. It is not directly internet-facing, and there is no evidence of active exploitation.

**Reasoning:** This is serious because of its severity and valuable data. However, the actively exploited public web server is more immediately urgent due to exposure and current threat. The database is second in this example.

### Finding D — Medium on an isolated test machine
A medium-severity vulnerability affects an isolated test machine with no valuable data and no external access.

**Reasoning:** The stated context makes the real risk comparatively low. It can wait for the normal remediation cycle.

### Example triage order

| Order | Finding | Context | Decision |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | High — public web server | Internet-facing, actively exploited, patch available | Fix first / urgent |
| 2 | Critical — internal customer database | Valuable data, internal, no active exploitation stated | Fix soon / high priority |
| 3 | Medium — isolated test machine | Isolated, low-value, no external access | Normal cycle / can wait |
| Exclude or validate | Critical — software not installed | Finding is not applicable as described | Remove from applicable list after confirming |

The raw severity list might look like “critical, high, critical, medium.” The context-based order places the high finding first, the relevant critical finding second, the medium finding third, and the inapplicable critical finding outside the actionable list.

**Context, not severity alone, determines real risk.**

## 4. Triage well: justify and communicate decisions

A ranking without explanations is not enough. Good triage communicates why each finding occupies its place.

Examples:
- “This is first because it is actively exploited on an internet-facing system, so exposure is high and the threat is immediate.”
- “This is lower despite critical severity because the affected software is not installed; applicability needs to be confirmed.”
- “This is low priority because the system is isolated and holds no valuable data.”

The reasoning makes the ranking a professional judgement rather than a guess. It also makes the decision understandable to managers, operations teams, and other analysts.

The lesson notes that two thoughtful analysts may order middle-priority findings somewhat differently. What matters is that the factors are considered and the decision is justified. The rationale is often more important than the exact ordering.

### Be transparent about trade-offs

Triage is a current prioritisation, not a permanent ranking. When lower-priority issues wait, the organisation is consciously accepting some risk for now. Some vulnerabilities may need compensating controls rather than immediate patching. The order should change if circumstances change—for example, if a previously quiet vulnerability becomes actively exploited or a system becomes internet-facing.

A professional triage summary should state:
- What is being fixed first and why.
- What is being deferred and why.
- What risk is being accepted temporarily.
- Which compensating controls may be needed.
- What new evidence would cause the order to change.

## 5. Connection to the broader security lifecycle

Vulnerability triage brings together concepts from this learning phase:

- **Day 67 — Risk management:** assess likelihood and impact, prioritise, and choose a response.
- **Day 77 — Defence in Depth:** use additional safeguards where immediate remediation is not possible.
- **Day 78 — Vulnerability Management / CVSS:** understand severity and the vulnerability-handling lifecycle.
- **Day 79 — Patching:** account for patch availability, operational friction, and the patch window.

Triage applies risk management to vulnerability findings. Outcomes may include validating a finding as not applicable, remediating urgently, scheduling normal remediation, applying compensating controls, or consciously accepting risk for a documented period.

This is also a common professional and interview skill: being presented with a list of vulnerabilities and asked what should be fixed first—and why.

## 6. Practical triage workflow

1. **Validate applicability.** Confirm the affected software or component exists in the environment.
2. **Start with severity.** Record CVSS severity as an initial signal.
3. **Assess exposure.** Determine whether the system is internet-facing, internal, or isolated.
4. **Assess impact.** Identify the system's business role and the value or sensitivity of its data.
5. **Check exploitation.** Determine whether active exploitation is known.
6. **Check remediation options.** Establish whether a patch is available and practical, or whether compensating controls are needed.
7. **Rank findings.** Build a fix-first order from the combined risk factors.
8. **Document rationale and trade-offs.** Explain each placement and what is being deferred.
9. **Reassess when conditions change.** New exploitation evidence, changed exposure, or new business context can alter priority.

## 7. Security+ revision notes

### Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Vulnerability triage | Assessing and prioritising vulnerability findings according to risk |
| Severity | General measure of how serious a vulnerability is, often represented by CVSS |
| Exposure | How reachable a vulnerable system or component is to an attacker |
| Impact | Potential loss or harm if a vulnerability is exploited |
| Active exploitation | Evidence that attackers are currently exploiting the vulnerability |
| Applicability | Whether the affected software or component actually exists in the environment |
| Compensating control | Alternative safeguard used to reduce risk when direct remediation is not feasible |
| Risk acceptance | Conscious decision to tolerate risk, usually documented and revisited |

### Questions to review

**Why is severity not enough to prioritise vulnerabilities?**  
Because severity does not include all local context. Exposure, asset value, active exploitation, applicability, and patch feasibility can change real risk.

**Why might a high-severity vulnerability be fixed before a critical one?**  
If the high finding is on an internet-facing system and actively exploited, it may present a more immediate risk than a critical finding on a less exposed internal system.

**What should happen to a critical finding for software the organisation does not use?**  
Validate that the software is truly absent and the finding is not applicable. Then remove it from the actionable list or mark it accordingly.

**What makes a triage decision defensible?**  
A clear explanation linking priority to severity, exposure, impact, exploitation evidence, and remediation options.

**Why should triage be revisited?**  
Exposure, exploitation activity, asset importance, and remediation availability can change over time.

## 8. Final takeaway

Vulnerability triage is the judgement of deciding what to fix first when the organisation cannot fix everything at once. CVSS severity is a useful starting point, but real risk depends on context: whether attackers can reach the vulnerable system, how valuable it is, whether exploitation is active, and whether a fix is practical.

A strong triage is more than a list. It includes a reasoned, defensible explanation for each placement, makes deferred risk explicit, identifies compensating controls when needed, and is updated as circumstances change. This is risk management applied directly to vulnerability findings—and a practical skill security analysts use every day.
