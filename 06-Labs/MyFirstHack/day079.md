## 1. Why patching is not as easy as it sounds

A patch is a software update released by a vendor to correct a problem, including a security vulnerability. When a vulnerability is discovered and a fix is made available, installing the patch is the most common way to remediate that specific weakness.

For one personal computer, updating may be a simple click. In an organisation, patching can involve thousands of servers, endpoints, applications, and specialist devices. The challenge is the difference between a patch being available and that patch being successfully applied across every affected asset. During the gap, systems may remain vulnerable to attacks that target a known weakness.

Patching is therefore not only a technical task. It is an operational process that must balance security urgency with service reliability and business requirements.

## 2. Why patching is difficult in practice

### Patches can break things

A patch changes software. Even a security fix may unintentionally break a feature, conflict with another application, or change behavior relied on by the organisation. This is why teams test patches before deploying them widely. Testing helps reduce the chance of an outage, but it takes time and can leave the vulnerability open during the test period.

The trade-off is real: deploying immediately may risk disruption, while waiting to test may increase exposure. Testing should be efficient and should not become an indefinite reason to delay a critical fix.

### Patching can cause downtime

Some updates require a service restart or system reboot. Interrupting a hospital system, factory control environment, or banking service may be disruptive and costly. Teams must plan maintenance windows, coordinate stakeholders, and make sure services recover correctly. These requirements can delay deployment.

### Scale and asset inventory

Large organisations manage many systems and software products. They need an accurate inventory showing what assets exist, what software and versions they run, which patches apply, and which updates have been installed.

An incomplete inventory means a device may be missed. The practical principle is: **you cannot reliably patch what you do not know you have.** Asset discovery and inventory are foundational parts of patch management.

### Legacy and unpatchable systems

Some software is no longer supported, so the vendor may not release fixes. Other systems cannot be taken offline, or are specialised devices where an update may affect certification or required functionality. Dependencies can also make patching one component require changes elsewhere.

When no applicable patch exists or applying it is unsafe, the vulnerability remains. The organisation must reduce exposure with compensating controls, document the remaining risk, and consider an upgrade or replacement.

## 3. The patch window: a race against attackers

The **patch window** is the period between a vulnerability becoming known or a patch becoming available and the affected system actually being patched.

When a vulnerability is publicly disclosed and a patch is released, defenders work to deploy it. Attackers can also study the disclosure and look for systems that have not yet been updated. During this period, the weakness is known and potentially exploitable.

The faster the organisation applies the patch, the smaller the window of exposure. The lesson notes that attackers may begin scanning for or exploiting serious vulnerabilities within hours or days—sometimes faster than organisations can complete their normal testing and deployment process.

For critical issues, particularly remote code execution (RCE) vulnerabilities or flaws affecting internet-facing systems, organisations may need to fast-track remediation. They may decide that the security risk of waiting is greater than the risk of patch-related disruption.

## 4. Zero-day vulnerabilities

A **zero-day vulnerability** is being exploited before a patch is available. The vendor has had “zero days” to provide a fix from the defender's perspective. Because there is no patch yet, defenders cannot simply install one to close the vulnerability.

Until a fix becomes available, the organisation may need to reduce exposure through mitigations, such as restricting access, disabling a vulnerable feature where practical, monitoring for exploitation, or following vendor guidance.

A key distinction:

| Situation | Patch available? | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Known, patchable vulnerability | Yes | Prioritise, test proportionately, deploy, and verify |
| Zero-day | Not yet | Mitigate, reduce exposure, monitor, and patch once available |
| Unsupported / unpatchable system | Maybe not | Apply compensating controls and manage residual risk |

The lesson emphasises that many attacks exploit known vulnerabilities for which fixes already exist but have not been applied. Those cases are often more preventable than zero-day attacks.

## 5. How organisations patch well

### Test, then deploy — but quickly

A practical process is to test an update on a small, representative group of systems, check for breakage, and then deploy it more broadly. The goal is to lower the risk of disruption without testing so slowly that systems remain vulnerable for weeks.

A simplified workflow:

1. Identify affected assets and the relevant patch.
2. Assess severity, exposure, and evidence of exploitation.
3. Test on a limited set of systems when appropriate.
4. Deploy to the remaining affected systems.
5. Verify patch installation and service health.
6. Record exceptions and systems that remain unpatched.

### Prioritise by risk

Not all patches have equal urgency. Critical updates for actively exploited vulnerabilities, especially on exposed systems, may be fast-tracked or deployed outside the normal schedule. Less urgent updates can follow routine maintenance cycles.

Risk-based patching directs effort toward the vulnerabilities and assets that pose the greatest risk. Consider severity, active exploitation, internet exposure, asset criticality, and whether mitigations exist.

### Use regular patch windows

Scheduled maintenance windows allow teams to coordinate updates, prepare recovery plans, and communicate with users. Routine updates can follow these windows. A critical security issue may require an emergency, out-of-cycle deployment rather than waiting for the next planned window.

### Use compensating controls when patching is not possible

When a system cannot be patched, the organisation should not leave it without safeguards. Compensating controls reduce risk while the weakness itself remains.

Examples from the lesson:
- Isolate the system on a separate network segment.
- Restrict access to only required users and systems.
- Add monitoring to detect suspicious activity or exploitation attempts.

These measures do not remove the vulnerability. They reduce the paths available to attackers, improve detection, and help manage the remaining risk. The organisation should document the exception and consider replacement or upgrade where feasible.

## 7. Practical scenario

An organisation discovers that an internet-facing server has a serious vulnerability. A vendor patch is available, and attackers are scanning for vulnerable systems.

The security team identifies affected assets and checks the urgency. Because the server is exposed and the vulnerability is serious, the patch is prioritised. Operations tests it on a representative system, prepares a recovery plan, and deploys it quickly. The team then verifies the installed version and confirms that the service is functioning.

A different legacy device cannot be patched because its vendor no longer supports it. The organisation isolates it, restricts access, monitors its traffic, documents the remaining risk, and plans replacement. In the first case, the weakness is remediated by patching. In the second, compensating controls reduce risk without fixing the underlying vulnerability.

## 8. Important terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Patch | Vendor update that corrects a software issue or vulnerability |
| Patch management | Identifying, prioritising, testing, deploying, and verifying patches |
| Patch window | Time a known vulnerability remains unpatched after disclosure or patch availability |
| Zero-day | Vulnerability exploited before a patch is available |
| Risk-based patching | Prioritising patches based on the risk posed by the flaw and affected asset |
| Maintenance window | Planned period for updates or system changes |
| Compensating control | Alternative safeguard used when the primary fix cannot be applied |
| Legacy system | Older system that may be difficult to update, support, or replace |
| Asset inventory | Record of systems and software an organisation needs to manage |

## 9. Security+ revision questions

**Why is patching hard at scale?**  
Patches can break functionality, cause downtime, require coordination across many assets, and may not be available for legacy systems. An incomplete inventory can also cause assets to be missed.

**What is the patch window?**  
The time between vulnerability disclosure or patch availability and the patch being applied. Attackers may exploit affected systems during this period.

**How is a zero-day different from a known vulnerability with a patch?**  
A fix is available for the known, patchable vulnerability. A zero-day is exploited before a patch exists, so defenders need mitigations until the vendor releases a fix.

**Why test a patch?**  
To detect compatibility or operational problems before wide deployment. Testing must be balanced against the risk of leaving the vulnerability exposed.

**What can an organisation do when it cannot patch?**  
Apply compensating controls such as isolation, access restrictions, and monitoring; document residual risk and plan a longer-term solution.

**What is risk-based patching?**  
Prioritising urgent patches, such as those for critical and actively exploited flaws on exposed systems, while handling routine updates through normal patch cycles.

## 10. Final takeaway

Patching is one of the highest-value security practices, but it is more than clicking “update.” At organisational scale, teams must account for system stability, downtime, asset inventory, dependencies, and unsupported devices.

The patch window is a race between defenders and attackers. Mature patch management uses timely testing, risk-based prioritisation, regular maintenance windows, and expedited deployment for critical issues. If patching is not possible, compensating controls—such as network segmentation, restricted access, and monitoring—reduce exposure even though the vulnerability remains.
