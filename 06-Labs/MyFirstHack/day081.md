## 1. Lesson overview: seeing yourself as an attacker would

On Day 26, the digital-footprint exercise introduced the idea that traces of a person can be scattered across the internet. Returning to that exercise with a stronger understanding of security changes the perspective. The aim is now to examine the same footprint as an attacker might: what can be discovered, how could those details be combined, and what exposure can be reduced?

**OSINT** stands for **open-source intelligence**. It means gathering information about a target from publicly available sources. In this context, “open-source” means openly available information, not open-source software. Examples include social media, public websites, search engines, public records, and leaked data that is circulating.

OSINT is a recognised information-gathering discipline used in security and also by investigators, journalists, recruiters, and others. When it is used to research a person or organisation before an attack, it forms part of the reconnaissance stage of the kill chain (Day 71).

Doing OSINT on yourself has two purposes. First, it makes reconnaissance concrete by showing how much may be learned from public sources before any technical attack begins. Second, it reveals personal exposure so that you can reduce or manage it. The goal is not paranoia; it is to build an accurate picture of what is findable and act on it.

## 2. How attackers use OSINT

Reconnaissance is purposeful information gathering. Attackers use OSINT to prepare and personalise attacks, and understanding those purposes helps distinguish meaningful exposure from information that is merely visible.

### 2.1 Targeted phishing and spear-phishing

A major use of OSINT is to make phishing messages more convincing. By researching a target, an attacker may discover the target's employer, colleagues, interests, recent life events, or services they use. A message that refers to a real employer, colleague, or service can appear more believable than a generic message.

This targeted form of phishing is often called **spear-phishing**. OSINT gives the attacker details that can be used to personalise the lure. The lesson connects this with phishing from Day 15 and the breach examined on Day 73, where employees were researched before a phishing email was crafted.

### 2.2 Security-question answers and password resets

Information shared publicly—such as a pet's name, hometown, or school—may reveal answers to security questions. If a service uses those details for account recovery, an attacker who discovers them may try to abuse that recovery process.

The key lesson is that information that seems harmless in a social context may become useful in an authentication or impersonation context.

### 2.3 Account targeting and broader attack planning

Knowing an email address and the services a person uses can help an attacker identify where to direct attempts. Information about an organisation's structure, technologies, and staff may also help plan a broader attack.

The danger often comes from **combining many small details**. A job title, gym, pet's name, birthday, and public post may each seem insignificant alone. Together, they can form a profile useful for impersonation, social engineering, and targeted attacks.

When reviewing your own footprint, ask not only “What is publicly visible?” but also **“What could someone do with this information?”**

## 3. What information may be findable?

The lesson highlights four main categories to check. Together, they provide a practical checklist for reviewing a personal digital footprint.

### 3.1 Social media

Social media is often one of the richest sources of personal information. Your profiles—and public posts made by friends or family—may reveal:
- Where you live or work.
- Relationships and social connections.
- Interests, routines, and life events.
- Photos that show locations or habits.
- Opinions and other personal details.

Even if your own profiles are private, you may appear in other people's public posts. Therefore, review not only your profile settings but also the information you voluntarily share and the public content in which you are tagged or mentioned.

### 3.2 Data breaches

Services have suffered data breaches, and leaked information such as email addresses, passwords, and personal details may circulate or be searchable. An email address appearing in a known breach can tell attackers where to target you. A leaked password is especially risky if it was reused on other services.

The lesson recommends checking whether your email appears in known breaches through legitimate services designed for that purpose. If it does, check password exposure carefully and ensure that any leaked password is no longer in use. Password reuse makes one service's breach more damaging to other accounts.

### 3.3 Public records and search results

Search engines may surface old accounts, forum posts, professional profiles, mentions on websites, and public records. Depending on the country and record type, public information may include property records, electoral rolls, or company filings.

A basic self-review can include searching your own name, email address, and usernames to see what is openly associated with you. The lesson's point is that a search can reveal more than people expect.

### 3.4 Metadata

Metadata is information embedded in or associated with a file. Photos may contain location data, while documents may include author names or other details that were not obvious from the visible content.

Before sharing photos and documents, consider what information they may reveal beyond the content you intended to publish.

**Checklist:** social media; data breaches; public records and search results; metadata.

## 4. Turning OSINT into defence

The purpose of self-OSINT is to take action, not simply to feel alarmed. Once you understand your exposure, you can reduce what is controllable and manage what remains.

This connects with threat modelling from Day 20: rather than thinking about privacy in vague terms, you can consider realistic ways that the information about you might be used.

### 4.1 Reduce unnecessary social-media exposure

Review privacy settings and restrict information that does not need to be public. Avoid oversharing details that reveal your location, routine, or answers to security questions. Remember that information may also be visible through other people's posts, so your own account settings are not the whole picture.

### 4.2 Check breach exposure and stop password reuse

Check whether your email address appears in known breaches using a legitimate breach-checking service. If a password may have been exposed, ensure it is no longer used. Change affected passwords and avoid reusing passwords across services.

The key defensive lesson is not to assume that an old leaked password is harmless. If it was reused, the exposure may affect more than the service where the breach occurred.

### 4.3 Review old accounts and search results

Close old accounts that you no longer use where possible. An unused account can still represent exposure or a potential attack surface. Search your name, email address, and usernames to understand what results are publicly associated with you.

Not everything in search results can necessarily be removed, but knowing what appears helps you make informed choices.

### 4.4 Be mindful of metadata

Consider what photos and documents reveal before sharing them. Location information in a photo or author details in a document may expose more than the visible content suggests.

### 4.5 Manage exposure that cannot be removed

You cannot necessarily erase your entire digital footprint. You may appear in other people's posts, public records, or past breaches. In those cases, awareness becomes part of the defence.

For example, a phishing message that mentions your employer or a colleague may feel highly personal. However, OSINT awareness reminds you that these details can sometimes be found publicly. Personalised information alone should not be treated as proof that a message is legitimate.

The mature outcome is to reduce what you can, understand what remains, and avoid being easily persuaded by attacks built from public information.

## 5. A practical self-OSINT checklist

Use this checklist as a personal review. It follows the categories and defensive actions described in the lesson.

- [ ] Review the privacy settings of social-media accounts.
- [ ] Check whether public posts reveal your workplace, location, routines, relationships, or life events.
- [ ] Consider information about you that appears in friends' or family members' public posts.
- [ ] Search your name and common name variations.
- [ ] Search your email address and usernames to see what is publicly associated with them.
- [ ] Review old accounts and close those you no longer need, where possible.
- [ ] Check whether your email appears in known data breaches using a legitimate service.
- [ ] If a password may have been exposed, ensure it is no longer in use and is not reused elsewhere.
- [ ] Review photos and documents before sharing them for potentially revealing metadata.
- [ ] For each exposure, ask: “What could an attacker do with this information?”
- [ ] Decide which exposures can be reduced and which must instead be managed through awareness.

This is a self-review of information about yourself. The lesson's defensive purpose is to understand and reduce your own exposure.

## 6. The full circle: Day 26 revisited

Day 26 introduced mapping a digital footprint. This lesson revisits that exercise with a more developed security perspective.

The important change is not necessarily the information being examined; it is the way the information is interpreted. With an understanding of reconnaissance (Day 71), targeted phishing (Day 15), the breach examined on Day 73, threat actors (Day 74), and threat modelling (Day 20), separate public details can now be recognised as parts of a potentially exploitable profile.

The same online presence can therefore be seen in two ways:
- As a collection of ordinary posts, accounts, and public details.
- As information an attacker might combine to personalise a lure, attempt impersonation, or plan an attack.

Seeing the second perspective helps turn scattered traces into a clearer picture of exposure. It also supports a practical life skill: assessing what is visible about yourself, your family, or an organisation and deciding what to reduce or manage.

## 7. Key terms

- **OSINT (Open-Source Intelligence):** Gathering information about a target from publicly available sources.
- **Digital footprint:** The traces and information about a person that exist across online services and public sources.
- **Reconnaissance:** The information-gathering stage in which an attacker researches a target before acting.
- **Spear-phishing:** Targeted phishing that uses information about a particular person or organisation to make a lure more convincing.
- **Metadata:** Information associated with or embedded in a file, such as location data in a photo or author details in a document.
- **Data breach:** An incident in which information is exposed or accessed without authorisation; leaked information may later circulate.
- **Threat modelling:** Thinking systematically about threats and how they may affect a target, connected in this lesson to Day 20.

## 9. Final takeaway

OSINT is the gathering of information from publicly available sources. In an attack, it supports reconnaissance and can make phishing, impersonation, password-reset attempts, and broader planning more effective. Its risk often comes from combining many small details into a useful profile.

Reviewing your own footprint turns that knowledge into defence. Check social media, breach exposure, public search results and records, and metadata. Reduce unnecessary exposure, stop using exposed passwords, close old accounts where possible, and remember that a personalised message is not automatically trustworthy just because it contains real details about you.

The central question is:

**“What could someone do with the information they can find about me?”**

Answering it thoughtfully helps turn an unsettling discovery into informed control over your digital exposure.
