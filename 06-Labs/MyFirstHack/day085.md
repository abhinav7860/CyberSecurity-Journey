# Day 85 — Compliance
**Day 85 of 90 · Becoming Security+ Ready**  
**Added:** 2026-10-29

## Lesson Overview
Compliance means meeting the laws, regulations, standards, and rules that apply to an organisation, implementing required security measures, and being able to demonstrate that requirements have been met. Which rules apply depends on what an organisation does and where it operates.

Examples:
- Organisations handling personal data must follow applicable data-protection laws.
- Hospitals handling health records must follow health-data rules.
- Businesses accepting card payments must follow payment-card standards.

Compliance is a major driver of security work, budgets, controls, and hiring. Many security roles exist partly or wholly to achieve and maintain compliance.

## 1. Why Compliance Exists
The lesson gives three core reasons.

### Protecting people
Organisations hold personal, health, and financial information. Mishandling it can cause identity theft, fraud, or exposure of private details. Some organisations may underinvest in protection because the cost falls on them while much of the harm falls on individuals. Regulations make protection a legal obligation rather than an optional expense.

### Establishing a baseline
Without required standards, security could vary widely. Regulations and standards set a **minimum bar** that organisations subject to them must meet, raising the baseline and giving customers and patients more assurance.

### Accountability and trust
Compliance requires organisations to demonstrate that requirements are met through documentation, audits, and evidence. Organisations can face fines, penalties, or restrictions for non-compliance. Demonstration and accountability can also build trust among customers, partners, and regulators.

**In short:** compliance exists to protect people, establish a baseline, and create accountability and trust—even when the work feels like paperwork.

## 2. Main Categories of Regulation
The lesson recommends learning the categories rather than memorising every rule. Regulations vary by country and industry, but the main categories are recognisable by what they protect.

### Personal-data privacy laws
These protect people's personal information generally.

**GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation)** is the European Union's data-protection law. It sets rules for collecting, using, storing, and protecting personal data, includes significant penalties for breaches, and has influenced data-protection laws worldwide. Such laws help explain privacy notices, consent requests, and data-protection measures.

### Sector-specific regulations
These apply to particular industries and sensitive information.
- **HIPAA:** A United States healthcare regulation governing protection of health information.
- **SOX:** Cited in the lesson as an example focused on financial-reporting integrity.

The category is industry-specific requirements for information whose mishandling can have especially serious consequences.

### Payment and industry standards
These are requirements connected to specific activities.

**PCI-DSS (Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard)** contains security requirements for protecting cardholder data. The lesson describes it as an industry standard, not a government law, but mandatory for organisations handling credit-card payments and associated with consequences for failure to comply.

### Security frameworks and standards
These are structured sets of practices organisations use to organise and demonstrate security. Some are voluntary; others may be required.
- **ISO 27001**
- National cybersecurity frameworks

They provide recognised structures against which security can be organised or assessed.

| Category | Purpose | Lesson examples |
|---|---|---|
| Personal-data privacy laws | Protect personal information and privacy | GDPR |
| Sector-specific regulations | Protect sensitive industry data | HIPAA, SOX |
| Payment / industry standards | Protect cardholder data | PCI-DSS |
| Security frameworks | Organise and demonstrate security practices | ISO 27001, national frameworks |

Recognising the category helps explain what a rule is for and who or what it applies to.

## 3. Compliance Is Not the Same as Security
Compliance and security overlap, but they are not identical.

### Compliance does not guarantee security
An organisation can meet every requirement and pass an audit yet still be insecure. Compliance is often a minimum and somewhat generic baseline. Passing an audit does not prove that every organisation-specific risk has been addressed. An organisation that only checks boxes may leave real threats unresolved.

### Security does not guarantee compliance
An organisation may have strong security controls but fail a specific requirement—for example, missing required documentation. Compliance requires particular requirements to be fulfilled in particular ways, not merely a generally good security outcome.

### The box-ticking trap
When an organisation focuses on passing an audit instead of addressing its actual risks, compliance can become a substitute for security and create a false sense of safety.

> **Compliance is a floor, not a ceiling.**

Compliance is a minimum to meet, not the endpoint. This connects to risk-based thinking from Day 67.

### Hold both truths
Compliance is valuable:
- It raises the baseline.
- It creates accountability.
- It protects people.
- It can drive security measures organisations might otherwise underinvest in.

Compliance is also insufficient alone:
- It is a minimum, not the whole security goal.
- Requirements may be generic.
- Box-ticking can leave actual risks unresolved.

A mature security professional uses compliance as a baseline and driver while continuing to assess real threats. Ideally, compliance is achieved as part of genuine security—not pursued as the final goal.

## 4. Why Compliance Matters for a Security Career
Compliance is directly relevant to security work and hiring. Many roles involve:
- Implementing required controls
- Preparing for and passing audits
- Documenting that requirements are met
- Maintaining compliance
- Understanding priorities shaped by regulatory obligations

Many organisations justify security investment because they must comply with a particular requirement. Even technical roles operate within the compliance requirements the organisation must meet.

### What to carry into the field
1. Recognise the categories: privacy laws, sector-specific rules, payment standards, and security frameworks.
2. Understand that compliance helps explain an organisation's security priorities.
3. Know that compliance drives security budgets, controls, and hiring.
4. Remember that compliance is a valuable floor, not the final security goal.
5. Be able to discuss compliance clearly in interviews and workplace conversations.

## Important Distinctions
- **Compliance:** Meeting applicable rules and demonstrating that they are met.
- **Security:** Protecting systems and information against threats.
- **Audit:** Examining and assessing whether requirements are met.
- **Evidence:** Documentation or other proof supporting compliance.
- **Baseline:** Minimum required protection.
- **Framework:** Structured practices used to organise or demonstrate security.
- Passing an audit does not prove every risk is addressed.
- Strong security does not automatically satisfy every specific requirement.
- Compliance is a floor, not a ceiling.
