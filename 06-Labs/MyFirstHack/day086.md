## Lesson Overview
Risk management is the organisational discipline of identifying what could go wrong, deciding how much each risk matters, and choosing what to do about it.

> **Security does not exist for its own sake. It exists to manage risk.**

Every control, defence, and compliance requirement ultimately helps reduce risk to an acceptable level.

You first encountered risk as **likelihood × impact** on Day 67. You have since applied it to threat modelling (Day 20), vulnerability triage (Day 80), and deliberate risk acceptance. Day 86 zooms out to show risk management as the wider organisational process connecting those activities.

Cyber risk is one part of an organisation's overall risk picture, alongside financial, legal, operational, and other risks. The security team's work is, in essence, managing the organisation's cyber risk.

### The real goal: acceptable risk
The goal is not to eliminate every attack or vulnerability. Total protection is impossible and prohibitively expensive. Organisations aim to reduce significant risks to an **acceptable level**, given limited resources and competing priorities. They reduce the most important risks enough, at a justifiable cost, so the remaining risk is tolerable.

## 1. The Risk Management Cycle
Organisational risk management is a structured, repeating cycle. It resembles vulnerability management (Day 78), but is broader: it covers all organisational risks, not only vulnerabilities.

### 1. Identify risks
Work out what could go wrong:
- Identify valuable assets, such as data, systems, and operations.
- Identify what could threaten those assets.
- Consider vulnerabilities and possible attack paths.

This uses threat-modelling thinking from Day 20: what are the assets, who or what threatens them, and where are they vulnerable?

You cannot manage risks you have not identified. Identification is ongoing because new systems, threats, and vulnerabilities continually emerge.

### 2. Assess risks
Judge how significant each risk is using the Day 67 equation:

**Risk = Likelihood × Impact**

Assessment ranks risks, distinguishing major risks (high likelihood and high impact) from less significant ones. This is the same risk-based prioritisation used in the Day 80 vulnerability-triage capstone, now applied to the organisation's broader risk picture.

### 3. Respond to risks
Decide what to do about each risk. The lesson presents four response options: mitigate, accept, transfer, and avoid. The choice depends on the risk's significance and the cost or value of each response.

### 4. Monitor and review
Risk management is continuous. The landscape changes as new threats appear, the business changes, and controls age. A risk acceptable yesterday may not be acceptable tomorrow.

Organisations monitor risks and revisit decisions, cycling back through identification and assessment as circumstances change.

**Cycle:** Identify → Assess → Respond → Monitor and review → Repeat

Risk management is never “done”; it is a continuing process that keeps organisational risk under control as conditions change.

## 2. The Four Ways to Respond to a Risk
The lesson describes four core responses. Organisations choose one or a combination for each risk.

### Mitigate (reduce)
Take action to lower the risk's likelihood, impact, or both.

Examples from the course:
- Patching
- Hardening
- Access controls
- Defence in depth
- Training

Mitigation is common for significant risks that the organisation can reduce through controls. Much of security work is mitigation—but it is only one of the four options.

### Accept
Consciously decide to live with the risk because it is low enough to tolerate, or reducing it would cost more than the benefit is worth.

Risk acceptance is a deliberate, documented, and owned decision (Day 67), not negligence or failure to notice a risk. Organisations accept some minor risks so resources can be used on more significant ones.

### Transfer
Shift some of the risk or its financial impact to another party.

Examples:
- Cyber insurance, where an insurer takes on specified financial costs if a breach occurs.
- Outsourcing a risky function to a specialist provider, which may transfer some risk.

Transfer does not eliminate the underlying risk. It shifts some burden—especially financial burden—to another party.

### Avoid
Eliminate the risk by not doing the risky activity.

Examples:
- Discontinue an activity whose risk outweighs its value.
- Do not collect data that is unnecessary.
- Do not enter a risky market.

Avoidance removes the risk by removing its source. As the lesson puts it, data you do not hold cannot be breached.

| Response | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Mitigate** | Reduce likelihood and/or impact | Patch, harden, apply access controls |
| **Accept** | Consciously tolerate the risk | Document acceptance of a low risk |
| **Transfer** | Shift some risk or financial impact | Cyber insurance |
| **Avoid** | Remove the risky activity | Stop collecting unnecessary data |

Real risk management means choosing the response—or mix of responses—that best fits the risk's significance and the cost of the available options.

## 3. Security as a Business Decision
Security is a business decision made under real constraints. Organisations balance risk against cost and competing priorities.

### Limited resources
Organisations have finite money, time, and people. Security competes with product development, operations, growth, and other needs.

Every amount spent on security is an amount not spent elsewhere. Therefore, security investment must be justified:
- What risk does the measure reduce?
- How significant is that risk?
- Is the reduction worth the cost?

An organisation cannot simply “do all the security.” Beyond a point, additional security may cost more than the risk it removes is worth.

Risk management helps allocate limited resources where they reduce the most significant risk for the cost.

### Risk, not fear
Good security decisions are driven by assessed risk rather than the most frightening headline.

A high-likelihood, consequential risk—such as an unpatched system or a workforce susceptible to phishing—may deserve more attention than a dramatic but unlikely scenario.

This is the measured realism discussed in Day 74, now applied to the organisation's overall security investment. Defences should match actual risks, not simply the scariest imaginable threats.

### Risk appetite
**Risk appetite** is how much risk an organisation is willing to accept.

It is ultimately a leadership decision because it involves business trade-offs between risk, cost, and opportunity.

Different organisations may reasonably have different appetites:
- A bank or hospital may accept less risk because the stakes are high and invest more in protection.
- A small startup may accept more risk to move quickly with limited resources.

The security team's role is to identify and assess risks and recommend responses. Leadership decides how much risk to accept, what to mitigate, transfer, or avoid, and how much to spend.

This is why senior security work involves communicating risk to decision-makers as well as technical defence. Security serves the organisation's risk decisions.

## Important Distinctions
- **Risk is not only a vulnerability:** Cyber risk is one part of organisational risk, alongside financial, legal, operational, and other risks.
- **Risk management is continuous:** New threats, systems, and business changes can alter risk.
- **Accepting risk is not negligence:** It should be a conscious, documented, owned decision.
- **Transfer does not erase risk:** It shifts some burden or financial impact.
- **Avoidance removes the source:** The organisation stops the risky activity.
- **Risk appetite is a business decision:** Security professionals assess and advise; leadership determines acceptable trade-offs.
- **Zero risk is not the goal:** The goal is acceptable risk at a justifiable cost.

## Final Summary
Risk management gives purpose to the security work studied throughout this phase. Organisations identify risks, assess them using likelihood and impact, choose responses, and monitor continuously.

The four responses are **mitigate, accept, transfer, and avoid**. Security decisions must account for limited resources, competing priorities, and the organisation's risk appetite. Security teams assess and recommend; leadership decides what risk is acceptable.

**Remember:** Security is not about stopping everything. It is about managing risk wisely—reducing the most significant risks to an acceptable level at a justifiable cost.
