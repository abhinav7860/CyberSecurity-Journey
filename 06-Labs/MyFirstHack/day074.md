## 1. Introduction

Today I learned about **Threat Actors** — the people or groups behind cyber attacks.

Over the previous days, I learned how attacks progress through the Cyber Kill Chain, how attackers use techniques documented in MITRE ATT&CK, and how to analyse a breach by mapping the attacker's actions to different stages.

This lesson added an important missing part: **who is behind the attack and why they are attacking**.

A threat actor's identity and motivation can influence:

- What they target
- What techniques they use
- How much effort they are willing to spend
- How long they remain in an environment
- What their final objective is
- How defenders should prepare

> **Understanding the adversary helps make defence more focused instead of generic.**

## 2. What Is a Threat Actor?

A **threat actor** is a person or group that carries out, or may carry out, malicious activity against a target.

Instead of thinking about every attacker as simply a "hacker", it is more useful to understand the different types of adversaries.

For example:

- A person defacing a website for fun
- A criminal group deploying ransomware for money
- A nation-state trying to steal sensitive information
- An insider misusing legitimate access

These attackers can have very different:

- Motivations
- Capabilities
- Targets
- Techniques
- Levels of persistence
- Objectives

This makes identifying the likely threat actor an important part of security planning.

- **Who might attack this organisation?**
- **Why would they attack it?**
- **What are they likely to target?**
- **What capabilities might they have?**
- **How persistent might they be?**
- **What would they want to achieve?**

## 4. Main Types of Threat Actors

| Threat Actor | Main Motivation | Typical Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **Cybercriminals** | Financial gain | Ransomware, fraud, stealing and selling data |
| **Nation-State Actors** | Espionage, sabotage, influence | Highly capable, patient and well-resourced |
| **Hacktivists** | Ideology or a cause | Defacement, leaks and disruption |
| **Insiders** | Varies | Misuse legitimate access |
| **Script Kiddies** | Curiosity, mischief or notoriety | Use tools created by others |

The categories are useful for understanding behaviour, but they should not be treated as completely rigid definitions.

## 5. Cybercriminals

**Motivation:** Money

Cybercriminals are described in the lesson as the most common type of threat actor.

Their objective is usually financial gain.

Examples include:

- Ransomware
- Stealing financial information
- Fraud
- Selling stolen data
- Extorting victims

Cybercriminal groups can sometimes operate in an organised way, almost like businesses.

### Target Selection

Because their objective is profit, cybercriminals may target organisations that are:

- Easier to compromise
- More likely to pay
- Holding valuable information
- Profitable to attack

Their attacks can therefore be opportunistic.

If a target becomes too difficult or expensive to compromise, an attacker motivated by profit may choose another target.

### Defensive Perspective

This means basic security fundamentals can be extremely important against common cybercriminal activity.

Examples include:

- Security hardening
- Patching
- Strong passwords
- Security awareness
- Monitoring

---

## 6. Nation-State Actors

**Motivation:** Espionage, sabotage and influence

Nation-state actors are governments or groups working on behalf of governments.

The lesson describes them as highly capable and well-resourced adversaries.

Their objectives can include:

- Stealing government information
- Stealing military information
- Obtaining corporate intellectual property
- Espionage
- Sabotage
- Influence

They may be highly patient and can spend significant amounts of time pursuing a specific target.

### Advanced Persistent Threats

Nation-state activity is often associated with the term:

**Advanced Persistent Threat (APT)**

The lesson explains the terms as:

- **Advanced** — highly skilled
- **Persistent** — willing to continue pursuing a target for a long time
- **Threat** — represents a serious security threat

Unlike an opportunistic criminal looking for any profitable target, a nation-state actor may have a particular organisation and particular information in mind.

---

## 7. Hacktivists

**Motivation:** Ideology or a cause

Hacktivists carry out attacks to support a political, social or ideological position.

Their goal is generally not financial profit.

Possible activities include:

- Website defacement
- Document leaks
- Service disruption

Their capability can vary significantly.

What mainly distinguishes them is their **motivation**.

They may target organisations because they disagree with their actions, policies or positions.

---

## 8. Insiders

**Motivation:** Varies

An insider is someone who already has legitimate access to an organisation.

Examples include:

- Employees
- Contractors

An insider can misuse that legitimate access for malicious purposes.

Possible motivations include:

- Personal grievance
- Financial gain
- Taking information
- Helping another organisation
- Other personal reasons

### Unwitting Insider

An insider does not necessarily have to be intentionally malicious.

A legitimate employee can become involved because:

- They were tricked
- Their account was compromised
- They unknowingly performed a malicious action

### Why Insiders Are Dangerous

Insiders are particularly important because they may already have legitimate access.

This makes them different from an external attacker who first needs to get inside the environment.

---

## 9. Script Kiddies

The lesson uses **script kiddie** as a term for attackers who have limited technical understanding but use tools and techniques created by others.

Their motivations can include:

- Curiosity
- Mischief
- Notoriety

They may have relatively low technical capability, but they can still be numerous.

Automated tools can also continuously scan the internet for vulnerable systems.

This means even an organisation that is not particularly interesting to a sophisticated attacker can still encounter low-skill automated attacks.

---

## 10. Motivation Shapes the Attack

One of the most important lessons today was:

> **An attacker's motivation can influence the entire character of an attack.**

Knowing what an attacker wants can help defenders reason about what the attacker might do next.

Motivation can influence:

1. Target selection
2. Techniques
3. Persistence
4. Objectives
5. Level of effort

---

## 11. Cybercriminal Motivation

A financially motivated attacker generally wants a profitable outcome.

Their attacks may include:

- Ransomware
- Payment information theft
- Fraud
- Data theft

Because profit is the goal, attackers may prefer techniques that can be used at scale.

They may also abandon a target if attacking it becomes too difficult compared with other opportunities.

This shows how the economics of an attack can influence attacker behaviour.

---

## 12. Nation-State Motivation

A nation-state actor may have a specific target and a specific objective.

For example, the target may hold:

- Government information
- Military information
- Corporate intellectual property

The attacker may therefore spend much more time and effort pursuing the target.

| Cybercriminal | Nation-State Actor |
|---|---|
| Usually motivated by money | Often motivated by espionage, sabotage or influence |
| May target profitable victims broadly | May pursue specific targets |
| Often values efficiency | May spend significant time on one target |
| Financial objective | Information or strategic objective |

The important point is that **motivation affects behaviour**.

## 16. Realistic Threat Assessment

A security professional should avoid two extremes:

### Dismissing Threats

Assuming attackers are not interested in an organisation can lead to weak security.

### Assuming Every Attacker Is a Nation-State

Not every attack requires nation-state-level capabilities.

The lesson presents a balanced approach:

- Defend strongly against common threats
- Understand more capable threats relevant to the organisation
- Consider insider threats
- Recognise that organisations can sometimes become indirectly involved in larger operations

This is an example of realistic, risk-based security thinking.

## 17. Threat Actors and Different Organisations

Different organisations can face different threat landscapes.

| Organisation | Potential Threat Actor | Possible Motivation |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital | Cybercriminal | Financial gain / ransomware |
| Defence contractor | Nation-state | Espionage |
| Controversial organisation | Hacktivist | Ideological cause |
| Any organisation | Insider | Grievance, financial gain or other reasons |

These are examples rather than exclusive threat mappings.

The point is that security priorities should consider the adversaries that are realistically relevant to the organisation.

## 22. SOC Analyst Perspective

As someone learning toward a SOC role, this topic is important because a SOC analyst should not only look at isolated alerts.

An analyst can ask:

- What happened?
- What technique was used?
- Where is the activity in the attack lifecycle?
- What type of threat actor could this behaviour represent?
- What might the attacker be trying to achieve?
- What should I monitor next?

Threat actor analysis can therefore provide additional context when investigating alerts and incidents.
