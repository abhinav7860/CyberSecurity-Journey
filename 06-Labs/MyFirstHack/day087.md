## 1. Lesson Overview

Cybersecurity is not one job. It is a broad field containing roles with different responsibilities, skills, and strengths. Saying “I want to work in cybersecurity” is like saying “I want to work in medicine”: the next question is which part of the field.

The lesson maps three major areas:

- **Defensive security (Blue Team):** protecting organisations, monitoring for threats, detecting attacks, responding, and keeping systems secure.
- **Offensive security (Red Team):** legally and deliberately testing systems to discover weaknesses before real attackers do.
- **Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC):** managing security at organisational and policy level, including risk, compliance, audits, and advising leadership.

The variety is positive: people can contribute through technical work, investigation, communication, analysis, organisation, building, testing, or responding under pressure.

## 3. Defensive Security — The Blue Team

Defensive security is the largest part of the field in the lesson and the area most relevant to the likely starting point. Blue-team professionals protect organisations by monitoring systems, detecting attacks, investigating activity, and keeping systems secure.

### 3.1 SOC Analyst

A **Security Operations Centre (SOC) analyst** is the classic entry-level defensive role. Typical responsibilities:

- Monitor organisational systems for signs of attack.
- Investigate alerts.
- Triage serious activity from noise.
- Escalate or respond to genuine threats.

## 4. Offensive Security — The Red Team

Offensive security deliberately and legally tests systems to discover weaknesses before real attackers do. It is the part of cybersecurity most commonly imagined as “hacking,” but the lesson says it represents a smaller share of the field than defence.

### 4.1 Penetration Tester

A **penetration tester (pen tester)**:

- Works with permission.
- Attempts to break into an organisation's systems.
- Identifies vulnerabilities.
- Reports findings so they can be fixed.

The lesson connects this work to the kill chain and vulnerability concepts studied on Days 71 and 75, applied ethically and offensively in service of defence.

### 4.2 Related Offensive Roles

- **Red teamer:** simulates realistic adversaries to test an organisation's overall defence.
- **Vulnerability researcher:** discovers new flaws in software.

These roles are technically demanding and often not entry-level. Many people pursue them after building defensive or technical foundations.

**Key point:** Offensive security can be a future specialisation, but defence is where most of the work and jobs are; red-team roles are often destinations rather than starting points.

## 5. Governance, Risk, and Compliance — GRC

**GRC** stands for **Governance, Risk, and Compliance**. These roles manage security at organisational and policy level rather than focusing only on the technical front line.

Typical work:

- Ensure compliance obligations are met.
- Manage organisational security risks.
- Write policies.
- Conduct audits.
- Advise leadership.

A GRC analyst or risk analyst applies the course's compliance and risk-management concepts: assess risks, check whether controls meet requirements, prepare for audits, and help the organisation understand and manage security obligations.

These roles can suit people whose strengths include organisation, communication, analysis, and understanding the bigger picture—not only deep hands-on technical skills. The lesson highlights GRC as a genuine career direction and entry point for analytical and organisational strengths.

## 6. Where a Beginner Fits

The lesson's practical and encouraging answer is that the most common accessible entry point is an **entry-level defensive role**, typically a SOC analyst or similar junior Blue Team position.

### A realistic first goal

Rather than aiming immediately to become an elite hacker, penetration tester, or security architect, the lesson recommends:

> Land an entry-level defensive role and build from there.

Entry-level defensive work commonly involves monitoring, investigating, triaging, and responding—the skills the course has been developing.

### Why SOC is a natural starting point

The course foundation connects directly to SOC duties:

- Understanding how attacks and defences work.
- Examining logs and activity.
- Recognising unusual behaviour.
- Prioritising alerts and findings by risk.
- Recognising attacker techniques.
- Understanding incidents and response.

The lesson does not say a beginner must already be an expert. It says entry-level hiring looks for a real foundation, genuine interest and initiative, and demonstrable skills or projects.

### Portfolio evidence

The lesson identifies portfolio work as evidence that can help demonstrate a foundation. Examples include audits, investigations, analyses, and triage reports.

The goal is to show that you understand the fundamentals and can grow—not to present yourself as an expert before gaining professional experience.

## 7. Career Growth: The Entry Role Is a Launchpad

A SOC analyst role is not a ceiling. With experience, a professional may branch into incident response, threat analysis or threat intelligence, security engineering, security architecture, offensive security, or GRC and management.

The lesson encourages beginners not to decide their entire specialisation immediately. A practical path is to enter through an accessible role, gain real experience, discover which work is enjoyable, and then steer toward a specialisation.

## 12. Review Questions and Answers

### Q1. Is cybersecurity a single job?
**Answer:** No. It is a broad field with many roles, responsibilities, and skill requirements.

### Q2. What are the three major areas described in this lesson?
**Answer:** Defensive security (Blue Team), offensive security (Red Team), and Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC).

### Q3. What does a SOC analyst do?
**Answer:** Monitors systems for signs of attack, investigates alerts, separates serious activity from noise, and escalates or responds to genuine threats.

### Q4. Why is SOC analyst a natural entry point for this course?
**Answer:** The course covers logs, investigation, unusual activity, risk-based triage, attacker techniques, and incident response—the core areas connected to SOC work.

### Q5. What does an incident responder do?
**Answer:** Handles security incidents when they occur and applies incident-response processes.

### Q6. What does a threat intelligence analyst study?
**Answer:** Attackers and their techniques to help anticipate and defend against threats.

### Q7. How does a security engineer differ from a security architect?
**Answer:** A security engineer builds and maintains defences; a security architect designs the organisation's security structure.

### Q8. What does a penetration tester do?
**Answer:** With permission, attempts to break into systems to find vulnerabilities and reports them so they can be fixed.

### Q9. What is GRC?
**Answer:** Governance, Risk, and Compliance: managing security at organisational and policy level through risk management, compliance, policies, audits, and leadership advice.

### Q10. Does a beginner usually start as a penetration tester or security architect?
**Answer:** The lesson says these are often specialised or senior destinations. A common accessible start is an entry-level defensive role such as SOC analyst.

### Q11. Does starting in the SOC limit future career options?
**Answer:** No. It can be a launchpad toward incident response, threat intelligence, engineering, architecture, offensive security, GRC, or management.

### Q12. Must a beginner choose a specialisation immediately?
**Answer:** No. The lesson recommends gaining experience, discovering what you enjoy, and steering toward a specialisation over time.

### Q13. What do entry-level employers look for according to the lesson?
**Answer:** Foundational understanding, genuine interest and initiative, and demonstrable skills or projects—not expert-level mastery.

### Q14. How can a portfolio help?
**Answer:** It provides evidence of practical learning through projects, audits, investigations, analyses, and triage reports.

### Q15. What is the realistic first career goal highlighted by the lesson?
**Answer:** Land an entry-level defensive role and build experience from there.

## 14. Final Takeaway

Cybersecurity is a wide field with multiple career directions. Defensive security—especially SOC analyst work—is the most accessible and natural starting point described in this lesson. Offensive security and architecture are often specialised paths built on experience, while GRC offers a meaningful direction for people who enjoy risk, compliance, organisation, and communication.

**The practical goal is to enter through an accessible defensive role, demonstrate foundational skills through hands-on work and a portfolio, gain experience, and then grow toward the specialisation that fits best.**
