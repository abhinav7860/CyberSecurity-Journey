## What Exactly Happened?

Every breach ends with the same question:

> **What exactly happened?**

After alerts have fired and the immediate incident is contained, someone still needs to determine:

- Which systems were touched?
- What data was accessed or taken?
- How did the attacker get in?
- Where did they go?
- Are they still present?

Answering these questions is **network forensics**.

Network forensics reconstructs events from evidence left behind by a network, mainly:

- Logs
- Packet captures
- Alerts
- System and application records

The previous days focused on detecting threats. Forensics is about reconstructing what happened, usually after the incident, with enough detail and rigour to support a response, report, and potentially a legal case.

This brings the whole week together:

```text
Days 31-32 → Capture and read packets
Day 33     → Recognise attacks
Day 35     → Investigate traffic

Day 37     → SIEM
```

### Takeaway

Network forensics reconstructs an incident from evidence a network left behind — especially packet captures and logs.

Detection asks:

> **Is something happening?**

Forensics asks:

> **What exactly happened?**

# Building the Timeline

The central output of a forensic investigation is a **timeline** — a precise, ordered account of what happened and when.

The investigator gathers evidence from different sources and places events onto one timeline.

```text
Initial Access
      ↓
Lateral Movement
      ↓
Data Access
      ↓
Data Exfiltration
```

Different logs contribute different parts of the story.

### Authentication Logs
Show when attacker access began.

### Network Logs / Packet Captures
Show where the attacker connected and what traffic moved.

### System Logs
Show what happened on compromised machines and what was executed.

### Application Logs
Show what data or application functionality was accessed.

When these events are placed in chronological order, the attack becomes much easier to understand.

## Why Time Synchronisation Matters

If systems have different or incorrect clocks, events may appear in the wrong order.

This is why synchronised time is essential for forensic reconstruction.

The quality of the timeline depends heavily on logging decisions made before the incident.

### Takeaway

The main output of forensics is a timeline showing:

```text
Initial Access
      ↓
Lateral Movement
      ↓
Data Access
      ↓
Exfiltration
```

The timeline is built by combining authentication, network, system, application logs, and packet captures.

Synchronised clocks are essential.

# The Questions Forensics Answers

## 1. How Did They Get In?

This identifies the **initial access**.

Possible entry points include:

- Phished credentials
- Exploited vulnerabilities
- Compromised third parties
- Stolen devices

Finding the entry point is important because the organisation needs to close the weakness.

If the attacker is removed but the original entry point remains open, another attacker could use it again.

## 2. What Did They Do Once Inside?

This covers the attacker's actions and **lateral movement**.

Investigators want to know:

- Which systems were accessed?
- What was executed?
- Were privileges escalated?
- Did the attacker move toward more valuable systems?

Evidence can come from:

- System logs
- Network logs
- Packet captures

## 3. What Did They Access or Take?

This determines the **impact**.

Questions include:

- What data was accessed?
- What data was copied?
- Was personal information involved?
- What data was not accessed?

This can have legal and regulatory consequences. For example, if personal data was stolen, regulations such as GDPR may create breach-notification obligations.

## 4. Are They Still Here?

This looks for **persistence**.

Attackers may create ways to maintain access, such as:

- Backdoors
- New accounts
- Scheduled tasks

Closing the original entry point is not enough if another method of access remains.

## 5. When Did It All Happen?

This brings everything back to the timeline.

The investigation needs to establish:

- When the attack started
- When initial access occurred
- When lateral movement happened
- When data was accessed
- When data was exfiltrated
- When the attack was discovered

This also helps determine **dwell time** — how long an attacker remained inside before detection.

### Takeaway

```text
How did they get in?
        ↓
What did they do?
        ↓
What did they access or take?
        ↓
Are they still here?
        ↓
When did it all happen?
```

Together, these questions form the understanding of the incident.

# Evidence Handling and Chain of Custody

Forensic evidence may eventually be used in:

- Court
- Lawsuits
- Insurance claims
- Regulatory investigations

Because of this, evidence must be handled carefully.

## 1. Preserve the Original

The original evidence should not be investigated directly.

```text
Original Evidence
       ↓
Verified Copy
       ↓
Investigation
```

The original should remain untouched so that it can be used to verify the analysis later.

## 2. Prove the Evidence Wasn't Altered

Forensic copies can be verified using **cryptographic hashes**.

A hash works like a digital fingerprint.

```text
Original
   ↓
Hash
   ↓
Verified Copy
   ↓
Hash Again
   ↓
Compare
```

Matching hashes help demonstrate evidence integrity.

## 3. Chain of Custody

The **chain of custody** is the documented record of:

- Who handled the evidence
- When they handled it
- What they did with it
- Where it was stored

The goal is to maintain an unbroken history from collection onward.

## 4. Why This Matters Even Without a Court Case

At the beginning of an investigation, it may not be clear whether legal action will eventually be required.

Therefore, evidence should be handled properly from the start.

### Investigation vs Forensics

```text
Investigation
    ↓
Understanding what happened

Forensics
    ↓
Understanding what happened
+
Rigour that makes the findings defensible
```

### Takeaway

Core forensic evidence principles:

- Preserve the original
- Work on verified copies
- Use cryptographic hashes to verify integrity
- Maintain a documented chain of custody

# Forensics as a Career

**Incident Response and Digital Forensics (DFIR)** is a cybersecurity career path.

DFIR stands for:

> **Digital Forensics and Incident Response**

Specialist DFIR teams or firms may investigate major breaches.

Their work can include:

- Reconstructing attacks
- Investigating compromised systems
- Supporting legal proceedings
- Identifying how an attacker entered
- Finding what the attacker did
- Helping organisations recover
- Closing security gaps

The work combines technical investigation with communication because findings may need to be explained to security teams, executives, lawyers, regulators, and courts.

# Tools Used in Network Forensics

## Wireshark

Wireshark, used earlier in the week, is a core network forensics tool.

It can be used to examine packet captures and reconstruct network activity.

## SIEM

The SIEM from Day 37 is also useful for forensics.

Its searchable archive of normalised logs can be a starting point for reconstructing an incident timeline.

Other specialised tools can be used for:

- Forensic disk imaging
- Network traffic capture and indexing
- Compromised-system analysis

The skills already learned transfer directly into forensic work.

# Maya's Incident

Imagine Maya discovers something is wrong.

For example:

- Her payment processor notices unusual transactions.
- A customer reports that their information was misused.

Maya probably won't run a full DFIR investigation herself.

An investigator may be provided by her payment processor, insurer, or another security service.

If her systems and providers kept good logs, investigators can reconstruct:

- When the compromise started
- What systems were accessed
- What data was accessed
- Whether customer information was taken

If logging was poor, the investigation hits limits.

Maya may then be unable to clearly explain what happened to customers or regulators.

### Maya's main lesson

The defensive groundwork before an incident determines how well the organisation can understand and survive that incident.

```text
Segmentation
    +
Monitoring
    +
Logging
    ↓
Better Investigation
    ↓
Better Response
```

# Key Takeaways

- **Network forensics answers: "What exactly happened?"**
- It reconstructs incidents using evidence such as logs and packet captures.
- The central output is a precise timeline.
- Authentication logs can show initial access.
- Network logs and packet captures can show connections and data movement.
- System logs can show actions performed on compromised machines.
- Application logs can show what data or functionality was accessed.
- Synchronised clocks are essential for accurate timelines.
- Forensics asks how attackers got in, what they did, what they accessed, whether they remain, and when everything happened.
- Initial access needs to be identified so the entry point can be closed.
- Lateral movement helps show how attackers moved through the environment.
- Persistence must be investigated so attackers cannot simply return.
- Dwell time measures how long attackers were present before detection.
- Original evidence should be preserved and analysis performed on verified copies.
- Cryptographic hashes can help prove evidence was not altered.
- Chain of custody records who handled evidence and when.
- Wireshark is a core network forensics tool.
- SIEMs can provide a searchable source of logs for forensic investigations.
- DFIR is a cybersecurity career area combining digital forensics and incident response.
- Good logging and monitoring before an incident can determine how successfully the incident can be investigated afterward.
