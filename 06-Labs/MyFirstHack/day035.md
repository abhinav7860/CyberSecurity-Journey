# Investigate Real Traffic

## Day 35 of 90 — How Networks Actually Work

---

## Introduction

After learning how to capture packets, recognise network protocols, and read individual conversations, the next step is learning how to investigate a real packet capture.

A packet capture can contain thousands or even tens of thousands of packets. Reading every packet one by one is not an efficient way to investigate network traffic.

Instead, a security analyst follows a structured workflow to reduce a large capture into the small number of packets that actually matter.

The workflow covered in this lesson is:

```text
Orient
   ↓
Form a Question
   ↓
Filter
   ↓
Follow & Read
   ↓
Compare to Normal
   ↓
Document
```

The goal is not to find an attack in every capture.

The goal is to understand the evidence and report what the traffic actually shows.

---

# 1. The Analyst's Workflow

Investigating network traffic is not about reading every packet.

Instead, the analyst progressively narrows the investigation.

```text
Large Packet Capture
        ↓
     Orient
        ↓
 Form a Question
        ↓
      Filter
        ↓
 Follow & Read
        ↓
 Compare to Normal
        ↓
    Document
        ↓
      Report
```

Each step has a specific purpose.

## 1.1 Orient First

Before applying filters, first understand the capture as a whole.

Wireshark provides several useful tools under the `Statistics` menu.

Important orientation tools include:

- Protocol Hierarchy
- Conversations
- Endpoints
- IO Graph

The first questions to ask are:

- How many packets are in the capture?
- What is the time span of the capture?
- Which protocols are present?
- Which hosts are communicating?
- Which hosts are generating the most traffic?
- Is there anything unusual?

The purpose of this step is to understand the general shape of the capture before investigating individual packets.

---

## 1.2 Form a Question

After getting an overview, identify something that needs investigation.

Examples:

```text
Why is there so much DNS traffic?
```

```text
Who is this host communicating with?
```

```text
What is this unfamiliar protocol?
```

```text
Why are there hundreds of connection attempts?
```

```text
Why is one external IP receiving so much data?
```

Good investigation is question-driven. Instead of randomly searching through packets, identify a specific question and follow the traffic related to it.

---

## 1.3 Filter to the Thread

Once you have a question, use Wireshark display filters to narrow the capture.

Instead of thousands of packets, you want to reach dozens of relevant packets.

You can filter based on:

- IP address
- Host
- Protocol
- Port
- Conversation

Filtering makes the investigation manageable.

---

## 1.4 Follow and Read

After narrowing down the traffic, investigate the actual communication.

Look at:

- TCP conversations
- DNS queries
- TCP flags
- Source and destination addresses
- Packet timing
- Packet sizes
- Protocol information

For TCP traffic, Wireshark provides:

```text
Right-click packet
      ↓
Follow
      ↓
TCP Stream
```

This isolates the complete TCP conversation so it can be read as one communication.

The goal is to understand what actually happened instead of looking at isolated packets.

---

## 1.5 Compare to Normal

After understanding the traffic, compare it against a baseline of normal network activity.

Normal traffic can include:

- DNS lookups
- HTTPS connections
- Recognisable destinations
- Completed TCP handshakes
- Clean TCP teardowns
- Background network activity
- Encrypted traffic

Potentially suspicious patterns can include:

- Large numbers of SYN packets
- Many failed connections
- Unusual DNS queries
- Long random-looking domain names
- Regular connections to one external IP
- Unexpected plaintext traffic
- Unusual protocols
- Traffic to unfamiliar destinations

**Important:** Unusual traffic is not automatically malicious. It needs to be investigated in context.

---

## 1.6 Document

The final step is documentation.

Document:

- What was observed
- Which traffic was investigated
- Which packets or conversations were relevant
- Why the traffic was interesting
- What pattern it matched
- The evidence supporting the conclusion
- The analyst's confidence
- Whether additional investigation is required

A professional investigation should be understandable and reproducible by another analyst.

# 2. Wireshark Statistics Menu

The Statistics menu is one of the most useful areas of Wireshark when investigating an unfamiliar capture.

```text
Statistics
├── Protocol Hierarchy
├── Conversations
├── Endpoints
└── IO Graph
```

These tools provide a quick overview of the capture before individual packets are investigated.

---

# 3. Protocol Hierarchy

Path:

```text
Statistics → Protocol Hierarchy
```

Protocol Hierarchy displays the protocols found in the capture as a tree.

It provides information such as:

- Protocol names
- Packet counts
- Traffic percentages
- Protocol relationships

For example, a capture containing mostly:

```text
HTTPS
DNS
TCP
```

could represent ordinary web activity.

A large amount of ARP could indicate significant local network activity that may be worth examining.

An unfamiliar protocol can also become an investigation thread.

### Why Protocol Hierarchy is Useful

It quickly tells you the **character of the capture**.

Instead of manually inspecting thousands of packets, you can immediately understand which protocols dominate the traffic.

---

# 4. Conversations

Path:

```text
Statistics → Conversations
```

Conversations shows pairs of addresses that communicated with each other.

It can show:

- Source address
- Destination address
- Number of packets
- Number of bytes
- Communication volume

The results can be sorted to identify heavy communication.

For example:

```text
Internal Host
      ↓
Large amount of traffic
      ↓
External IP
```

could be worth investigating.

Similarly:

```text
One Host
   ↓
Hundreds of different addresses
```

could indicate scanning activity or simply normal activity from a busy server.

The traffic must be investigated before making a conclusion.

### Why Conversations is Useful

Conversations helps answer:

> Who is talking to whom?

It turns a large packet capture into a much easier-to-understand list of communicating hosts.

---

# 5. Endpoints

Path:

```text
Statistics → Endpoints
```

Endpoints displays the individual addresses involved in the capture.

It helps identify:

- Important hosts
- High-volume hosts
- External addresses
- Unusual destinations

For IP addresses, Wireshark can also provide geographic information.

An endpoint located in a country where an organisation has no expected business activity may deserve investigation.

However:

> An unusual geographic location is not proof of malicious activity.

It is simply a potential investigation thread.

---

# 6. IO Graph

Path:

```text
Statistics → IO Graph
```

The IO Graph displays traffic volume over time.

This makes traffic patterns easier to identify.

Normal background traffic may look relatively consistent:

```text
▁▂▁▂▁▂▁▂▁▂▁▂
```

A sudden traffic burst could look like:

```text
▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁█
```

Regular automated traffic could appear as:

```text
▂   ▂   ▂   ▂   ▂   ▂
```

Timing can therefore help identify:

- Traffic bursts
- Large transfers
- Regular connections
- Beacon-like patterns

---

# 7. What You Might Find

Not every packet capture contains an attack.

A capture may simply contain normal network traffic:

```text
DNS lookup
    ↓
HTTPS connection
    ↓
Recognisable service
    ↓
TCP communication
    ↓
Clean connection termination
```

The correct conclusion may simply be:

> The capture appears to contain normal web traffic with no obvious suspicious activity.

This is a complete and valid security finding.

The goal of an analyst is not to find something malicious at any cost. The goal is to accurately describe what the evidence shows.

---

# 8. Port Scanning

One possible pattern in a capture is a port scan.

A port scan may show one source sending SYN packets to many different ports in a short period.

Example:

```text
Source
  │
  ├── SYN → Port 21
  ├── SYN → Port 22
  ├── SYN → Port 23
  ├── SYN → Port 25
  ├── SYN → Port 80
  ├── SYN → Port 443
  └── SYN → Port 8080
```

Many of these connection attempts may receive RST responses.

This pattern can indicate port scanning.

The important point is to examine the actual traffic and supporting evidence before reaching a conclusion.

---

# 9. Suspicious DNS

DNS traffic can provide useful security clues.

Potentially suspicious patterns include:

- Very long domain names
- Random-looking subdomains
- Unusually high DNS activity
- Domains that appear machine-generated

For example:

```text
x7f3k9q2bv8.example.com
```

may look unusual and could deserve investigation.

However, an unusual DNS name does not automatically mean malware.

The analyst should investigate:

- What host made the request?
- How often was the query made?
- What IP address did it resolve to?
- What happened after the DNS lookup?
- Does the traffic match expected application behaviour?

---

# 10. Plaintext Traffic

Another possible finding is sensitive information being transmitted without encryption.

For example:

```text
Username
Password
Session information
Sensitive application data
```

If this information can be directly read from captured traffic when encryption should have been used, it may represent a security issue.

The investigation should determine:

- Which protocol is being used?
- Why is encryption not being used?
- What information is exposed?
- Who could potentially access the traffic?

---

# 11. Beaconing

Malware can sometimes communicate with an external system at regular intervals.

For example:

```text
10:00:00 → External IP
10:01:00 → External IP
10:02:00 → External IP
10:03:00 → External IP
10:04:00 → External IP
```

Precise timing can make the traffic stand out from normal human activity.

Small repeated connections to the same external destination can therefore become an investigation thread.

Again, this is a pattern requiring investigation, not automatic proof of malware.

---

# 12. Being Honest About Findings

One of the most important lessons is understanding uncertainty.

Security analysts should distinguish between different confidence levels.

## High Confidence

```text
This is definitely X.
```

Use this only when the evidence strongly supports the conclusion.

## Medium Confidence

```text
This looks like X, but I am not completely certain.
```

There is evidence supporting the conclusion, but additional investigation may be required.

## Low Confidence / Unexplained

```text
This traffic is unusual and I cannot currently explain it.
```

This is still a useful finding.

An analyst does not need to explain every unusual packet immediately.

---

# 13. Example of a Strong Finding

A weak finding would be:

```text
There is definitely an advanced attack happening.
```

if there is no evidence supporting the statement.

A stronger finding provides the actual observations:

```text
I believe this traffic represents a port scan because
one source sent SYN packets to approximately 200 ports
within two seconds, with many of the connections receiving
RST responses.
```

This explains:

1. What happened
2. What evidence was observed
3. Why the evidence matches the suspected activity

This makes the finding easier for another analyst to verify.

---

# 14. Avoiding Analyst Bias

After learning about network attacks, it can become tempting to interpret every unusual packet as malicious.

This can lead to incorrect conclusions.

Examples:

```text
Retransmission ≠ automatically an attack
Unknown protocol ≠ automatically malware
Unusual IP ≠ automatically malicious
DNS anomaly ≠ automatically DNS tunnelling
```

Many unusual events have normal explanations.

The correct approach is:

```text
Observe
   ↓
Investigate
   ↓
Compare with baseline
   ↓
Evaluate evidence
   ↓
Determine confidence
   ↓
Report
```

The goal is not to find an attack.

The goal is to report what the evidence supports.

# 17. Important Concepts Learned

### Orientation

Understand the capture before investigating individual packets.

### Question-Driven Investigation

Always have a reason for following a particular traffic pattern.

### Filtering

Reduce thousands of packets to the traffic relevant to the investigation.

### Conversation Analysis

Follow traffic between hosts to understand what actually happened.

### Baseline Comparison

Compare suspicious behaviour against known normal traffic.

### Evidence-Based Analysis

Base conclusions on what is actually visible in the capture.

### Confidence

Clearly communicate how certain the conclusion is.

### Documentation

Record observations and reasoning so another analyst can verify the finding.
