## 1. Introduction

Today, I learned about malware and the different families of malicious software. Malware means **malicious software**—software written to do harm.

I had already encountered malware-related concepts while investigating a suspicious script (Day 56), examining processes (Day 57), and learning about persistence mechanisms (Day 62). This lesson helped me organise those ideas into a clearer taxonomy.

People often use the word “virus” to describe all malware, but that is not precise. Malware families differ in how they spread and what they do.

The most useful way to classify malware is to ask two separate questions:

1. **How does it spread?** How does it get onto systems and propagate?
2. **What does it do?** What is its malicious purpose once it is there?

A single piece of malware can have both a spreading method and a purpose. For example, a worm could spread automatically and deliver a ransomware payload.

## 2. How Malware Spreads: Virus, Worm, and Trojan

### Virus

A **virus** attaches itself to a legitimate file or program. It spreads when the infected host is shared and run, usually requiring a person to open or execute the infected file.

Its defining feature is that it depends on a host file or program and human action to propagate.

### Worm

A **worm** spreads automatically across systems or networks without needing a host file or a person to run each copy. It may exploit vulnerabilities to move from one vulnerable machine to another.

Because a worm can propagate on its own, it can spread quickly through a network.

### Trojan

A **trojan**, or Trojan horse, disguises itself as something legitimate or desirable to trick a user into installing or running it. It relies on deception rather than attaching to a host file like a virus or spreading automatically like a worm.

The lesson connected this idea to the “just paste this to install our tool” trick from Day 60 and the disguised invoice attachment discussed in the Day 73 breach analysis.

### Quick Comparison

| Family | How it gets onto or spreads between systems | Defining feature |
|---|---|---|
| Virus | Travels with an infected file or program that someone shares and runs | Requires a host and usually human action |
| Worm | Propagates automatically across systems or networks | Self-spreading |
| Trojan | Tricks a user into installing or running it | Deception or disguise |

## 3. What Malware Does: Ransomware, Spyware, and More

These terms describe malware by its purpose. Its purpose is separate from how it arrived on a system.

### Ransomware

**Ransomware** encrypts a victim’s data and demands payment for the decryption key. It can make important files inaccessible and disrupt an organisation’s operations.

This connects to two earlier lessons:

- **Day 68 — Encryption Without the Math:** Ransomware weaponises encryption against the victim.
- **Day 66 — The CIA Triad:** By making data inaccessible, ransomware attacks availability.

The delivery method can vary. A ransomware payload might arrive through a trojan, a phishing message, or another method.

### Spyware

**Spyware** secretly gathers information without the user's knowledge. It may target passwords, financial details, personal information, or business secrets.

Spyware attacks **confidentiality**, which connects to Day 66. Its defining feature is stealth: it tries to remain hidden so that it can continue gathering information.

A **keylogger** is a specific type of spyware that records keystrokes, potentially capturing passwords and messages as they are typed.

### Botnet

A **botnet** is a network of computers controlled remotely by an attacker after they have been compromised. The attacker may use the machines to send spam, launch denial-of-service attacks, or carry out other large-scale abuse.

A computer may be part of a botnet without its owner realising it.

### Rootkit

A **rootkit** is designed to hide deeply within a system and conceal its own presence or the presence of other malware. This relates to **defence evasion**, which I studied in Day 72’s MITRE ATT&CK lesson.

### Adware

**Adware** displays unwanted advertising. The lesson describes it as generally less harmful than the other examples, although it can still be disruptive.

### Purpose-Based Comparison

| Malware type | What it does | Main concern |
|---|---|---|
| Ransomware | Encrypts data and demands payment | Availability and operational disruption |
| Spyware | Secretly gathers information | Confidentiality and data theft |
| Keylogger | Records keystrokes | Capturing typed information |
| Botnet malware | Enlists computers under remote control | Spam, denial-of-service, and other abuse |
| Rootkit | Hides malware or activity within a system | Concealment and defence evasion |
| Adware | Displays unwanted advertising | Unwanted or disruptive advertising |

## 4. The Modern Reality of Malware

### Malware can combine multiple characteristics

Real-world malware does not always fit into a single neat category. One specimen might spread like a worm, use a trojan-style disguise, hide like a rootkit, steal information like spyware, and eventually deploy ransomware.

This is why it is useful to ask both questions—how it spreads and what it does—instead of forcing it into one label.

### Delivery mechanism vs. payload

The **delivery mechanism** describes how malware gets onto a system or spreads. The **payload** describes what it does once it is running.

```text
Trojan-style delivery
        |
        v
Malware reaches the system
        |
        v
Ransomware payload runs
        |
        v
Victim's data becomes inaccessible
```

This distinction connects with the Cyber Kill Chain lessons from Days 71 and 73: delivery and installation are part of the attack's progression, while the payload's harmful activity may contribute to the attacker's objectives.

An analyst might therefore describe an incident as a trojan delivery that installed a ransomware payload.

## 5. Naming Malware Precisely

Using the correct terminology helps communicate what an incident means:

- **Ransomware:** Indicates malicious activity that denies access to data and demands payment.
- **Worm:** Indicates automatic propagation between systems.
- **Spyware:** Indicates covert information gathering.
- **Trojan:** Indicates deceptive software that tricks a user into running or installing it.
- **Rootkit:** Indicates software designed to conceal its presence or other malicious activity.

These labels help describe the threat more accurately than using “virus” as a general name for every type of malware.
