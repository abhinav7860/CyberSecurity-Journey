## Overview

Day 65 is the final day of the Linux phase and acts as a **capstone** for everything learned throughout this section.

The goal is not to learn a lot of new commands. Instead, the main goal is to bring the previous skills together and use them like an investigator.

In a real security incident, I may be given a system that is suspected to be compromised. My job is to examine the available evidence and reconstruct what happened.

The main questions are:

- Is the system compromised?
- What did the attacker do?
- How did they get in?
- What did they touch?
- Are they maintaining access?
- What evidence supports my conclusion?

This is a small version of **digital forensics and incident response**, which is an important part of SOC analyst and incident responder work.

# Investigator's Checklist

## 1. What is running that should not be running?

**Focus: Processes**

From Day 57, I learned how to inspect running processes.

During a forensic investigation, I should look for:

- Unfamiliar processes
- Processes consuming unusual amounts of resources
- Strange process names
- Processes running from unusual locations
- Processes that do not make sense for the system

The key question is:

> Is there anything running that should not be there?

A suspicious process by itself is not automatically proof of compromise. It needs to be correlated with other evidence.

---

## 2. What is the system talking to that it should not be talking to?

**Focus: Network Connections**

From Day 58, I learned how to investigate network connections.

I should look for:

- Unfamiliar external addresses
- Unexpected listening ports
- Possible backdoors
- Connections that appear unusual for the system
- Repeated connections that could indicate beaconing

The key question is:

> Is the system communicating with something it normally should not?

Network evidence becomes much more useful when it can be connected to a process, file, user, or timeline event.

---

## 3. What does the history show?

**Focus: Logs**

From Day 59, I learned how to inspect Linux logs.

Logs can help answer:

- When did something happen?
- Was there a failed login followed by a successful login?
- Was an account created?
- Were there authentication events?
- What events happened around the suspected compromise?

One useful pattern to investigate is:

```text
Failed Login
     |
     v
Another Failed Login
     |
     v
Successful Login
     |
     v
Suspicious Activity
```

This does not automatically prove an attack, but it can provide important evidence when combined with other findings.

---

## 4. What accounts exist that should not exist?

**Focus: User Accounts**

From Day 52, I learned how to investigate Linux users and accounts.

During the investigation I should ask:

- Are there unexpected accounts?
- Was a new account created?
- Who can log in?
- Who can become root?
- Does an account appear to have been created for persistence?

An unexpected account can be particularly important because attackers may create accounts to maintain access.

---

## 5. What is scheduled that should not be scheduled?

**Focus: Cron Jobs**

From Day 62, I learned about cron jobs and scheduled tasks.

During forensics I should check:

- User crontabs
- System cron locations
- Unexpected scheduled commands
- Scripts being executed automatically
- Suspicious scheduled tasks

A rogue cron job can be a form of **persistence**.

```text
Attacker gains access
       |
       v
Creates suspicious script
       |
       v
Creates scheduled task
       |
       v
Task runs automatically
       |
       v
Access/activity continues
```

---

## 6. What files are suspicious?

**Focus: Files**

From Day 56 and Day 54, I learned how to investigate suspicious files and search the filesystem.

Things to look for include:

- Recently modified files
- Files in unusual locations
- Files in `/tmp`
- Incorrect permissions
- Unexpected ownership
- Strange filenames
- Files that do not belong on the system

The important rule from the suspicious-file investigation is:

> Examine suspicious files without executing them.

The goal is to collect evidence safely.

# The Six Investigation Areas

| Area | Main Question | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Processes | What is running? | Running processes |
| Network | Who is the system communicating with? | Connections / ports |
| Logs | What happened and when? | Authentication and system events |
| Accounts | Who has access? | Users / privileges |
| Scheduled Tasks | Is access being maintained? | Cron / scheduled jobs |
| Files | What was created or modified? | Files / timestamps / permissions |

These six areas help answer the two major parts of an investigation:

```text
COMPROMISE
    |
    +--> How did they get in?
    +--> What did they do?
    +--> What did they touch?
    |
    v
PERSISTENCE
    |
    +--> New account?
    +--> Cron job?
    +--> Suspicious process?
    +--> Other access mechanism?
```

# Building a Timeline

One of the most important skills in forensics is creating a timeline.

Every finding can potentially have a time associated with it:

- Log timestamp
- File modification time
- Account creation time
- Process start time
- Network activity time

Instead of looking at each finding separately, I should arrange them chronologically.

## Example Timeline

```text
Failed Login Attempts
        |
        v
Successful Login
        |
        v
New Account Created
        |
        v
Suspicious File Appears in /tmp
        |
        v
Cron Job Added
        |
        v
Suspicious Process Starts
        |
        v
External Network Connection
```

This gives a much stronger picture than simply saying:

> "I found a suspicious file."

The timeline helps explain **what happened and in what order**.

# Evidence Correlation

Forensics is not about finding one suspicious thing and immediately declaring a compromise.

The stronger approach is to connect multiple pieces of evidence.

```text
Authentication Log
       |
       +----> Successful Login
                    |
                    v
              New Account
                    |
                    v
             Suspicious File
                    |
                    v
                Cron Job
                    |
                    v
            Running Process
                    |
                    v
          External Connection
```

Each individual finding provides some information.

Together, they can form a much stronger explanation.

---

# Evidence vs Assumption

A good investigator should separate:

### What the evidence proves

Example:

> A new user account was created.

### What the evidence suggests

Example:

> The account may have been created for persistence.

### What cannot currently be determined

Example:

> The original method used to obtain access could not be determined from the available evidence.

This is important because forensic investigations should be **evidence-backed**.

I should not claim something happened just because it seems likely.

---

# Confidence and Gaps

A professional investigation should clearly state:

- What evidence was found
- What conclusion the evidence supports
- How confident I am
- What evidence is missing
- What I could not determine

```text
Finding
  |
  v
Evidence
  |
  v
Reasoning
  |
  v
Confidence
  |
  v
Known Gaps
```

A professional conclusion may state that the system was compromised at an approximate time through a particular method, what actions were observed, how persistence was established, and what could not be determined from the available evidence.

The important part is that every statement should be supported by evidence.

# Safe Forensics Capstone

This capstone is designed to be performed safely.

I should **not use real malware or intentionally compromise a real system**.

Instead, I can create harmless evidence that looks similar to what an investigator might find during an incident.

Examples include:

- A benign practice file placed in a suspicious-looking location
- A harmless scheduled task
- Other harmless investigation markers

Then I can investigate the system as if it were a real incident.

Finally, I should clean up the practice evidence.

```text
Create Harmless Evidence
          |
          v
Treat It Like an Investigation
          |
          v
Collect Evidence
          |
          v
Build Timeline
          |
          v
Write Findings
          |
          v
Clean Up
```

This approach allows me to practice the **real investigative workflow without introducing actual danger**.

# Investigator Mindset

Instead of:

> "I found something strange, so it must be malicious."

I should think:

> "I found something unusual. What evidence can confirm or reject my suspicion?"

Instead of:

> "This probably happened."

I should think:

> "What evidence supports this conclusion?"

Instead of hiding uncertainty:

> "I don't know."

A professional report can say:

> "The available evidence was insufficient to determine this."

That is better than making an unsupported claim.
