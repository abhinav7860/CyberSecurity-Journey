## 1. Introduction

After learning about the defender's toolkit through topics such as the CIA Triad, threats and vulnerabilities, encryption, and hashing, I moved into understanding **how attacks actually happen**.

One important thing I learned is that a cyber attack is usually not one single event. It is a **sequence of stages**.

An attacker may spend time researching a target, preparing their tools, finding a way into the environment, establishing a foothold, communicating with their infrastructure, and finally achieving their objective.

The **Kill Chain** gives defenders a way to understand this sequence.

The main idea I took from this lesson is:

> **An attack is a chain of stages, and defenders can detect or disrupt the attack at different points in that chain.**

## 3. What Is the Kill Chain?

The **Kill Chain** is a model used to describe the different stages of a cyber attack.

The concept originally came from the military and was later adapted for cybersecurity.

The lesson explains that real attacks can happen over:

- Days
- Weeks
- Months

An attacker may first research the organisation, prepare an attack, find a way in, establish persistence, communicate with their infrastructure, and eventually perform their intended actions.

This means that defenders may have **multiple opportunities to detect and interrupt an attack**.

---

# 4. The Seven Stages of the Kill Chain

| Stage | What Happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Reconnaissance | The attacker researches the target. |
| 2. Weaponization | The attacker prepares the attack tool or payload. |
| 3. Delivery | The attacker sends or delivers the weapon to the target. |
| 4. Exploitation | The vulnerability or weakness is triggered. |
| 5. Installation | The attacker establishes a lasting presence. |
| 6. Command & Control | The compromised system communicates with attacker infrastructure. |
| 7. Actions on Objectives | The attacker performs the final intended actions. |

The stages form a chain:

```text
Recon
  ↓
Weaponization
  ↓
Delivery
  ↓
Exploitation
  ↓
Installation
  ↓
C2
  ↓
Actions on Objectives
```

---

# 5. Stage 1 — Reconnaissance

## What is it?

**Reconnaissance** is the stage where the attacker researches the target.

The attacker may try to discover:

- Employee names
- Email addresses
- Systems
- Technologies being used
- Publicly exposed information
- Potential weaknesses
- Other information that could help with the attack

The goal is to understand the target before attempting the attack.

### Connection to My Previous Learning

This connects directly to **Day 26 — Footprinting**.

During footprinting, I learned that information about an organisation can be gathered from publicly available sources.

The Kill Chain helped me understand why this information matters from an attacker's perspective.

```text
Information Gathering
        ↓
Understand Target
        ↓
Identify Potential Weaknesses
        ↓
Prepare Attack
```

### Defender Perspective

From a SOC perspective, reconnaissance can sometimes create early warning signs.

Possible indicators include:

- Scanning
- Unusual probing
- Repeated connection attempts
- Suspicious discovery activity

This means an attack may produce observable activity **before exploitation even happens**.

---

# 6. Stage 2 — Weaponization

## What is it?

After researching the target, the attacker prepares the tool they intend to use.

This is called **weaponization**.

The lesson describes this as preparing an attack by combining an exploit with a payload.

Examples can include:

- Malicious documents
- Malware
- Exploitation tools
- Malicious scripts

The important idea is that the attacker is preparing the actual weapon before delivering it.

### Connection to My Previous Learning

This connects with the malicious-file and scripting topics I studied earlier, including:

- **Day 56 — Investigate a Suspicious File**
- **Day 61 — Your First Bash Script**

These topics helped me understand that scripts and files can be legitimate, but can also be used as part of malicious activity.

```text
Target Research
      ↓
Prepare Exploit
      ↓
Attach / Prepare Payload
      ↓
Weapon Ready
```

---

# 7. Stage 3 — Delivery

## What is it?

**Delivery** is the stage where the attacker gets the prepared weapon to the target.

A common example is phishing.

Possible delivery methods include:

- Phishing emails
- Malicious links
- Malicious attachments
- Malware presented to the victim

The delivery stage often targets the **human side of security**.

For example:

```text
Attacker
   ↓
Malicious Email
   ↓
Victim
   ↓
Attachment / Link
```

The attacker is trying to get the prepared weapon into a position where it can be executed.

### Defender Perspective

A defender may be able to stop the attack here using controls such as:

- Email filtering
- Spam filtering
- Attachment inspection
- URL filtering
- User awareness

If the malicious content never reaches the target, later stages may never happen.

---

# 8. Stage 4 — Exploitation

## What is it?

**Exploitation** occurs when the attacker triggers the vulnerability or weakness.

This is the point where the prepared attack is used against the target.

The attacker may exploit:

- A software vulnerability
- A configuration weakness
- A security weakness
- A user interaction

The lesson connects this stage to **Day 67 — Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Risks**.

A vulnerability is a weakness that can be exploited.

The Kill Chain shows where that exploitation fits into the larger attack process.

```text
Delivered Weapon
       ↓
Weakness Found
       ↓
Exploit Triggered
       ↓
Malicious Code Executes
```

### Defender Perspective

One way to reduce exploitation opportunities is **patching**.

This connects to **Day 60 — Package Managers**, where keeping software updated was part of maintaining a secure system.

---

# 9. Stage 5 — Installation

## What is it?

After gaining execution, the attacker may try to establish a **lasting presence** on the system.

This is the **installation** stage.

The attacker may install:

- Malware
- A backdoor
- Other mechanisms that help maintain access

The important concept here is **persistence**.

### Connection to My Previous Learning

This connects to **Day 62 — Cron Jobs**.

I learned that scheduled tasks can be legitimate, but attackers can also abuse persistence mechanisms to make malicious activity continue after a system restart or logout.

The same general idea applies here:

```text
Initial Access
      ↓
Malicious Software / Mechanism
      ↓
Persistence
      ↓
Attacker Can Return
```

From a SOC perspective, suspicious persistence mechanisms can become important detection points.

---

# 10. Stage 6 — Command and Control (C2)

## What is it?

Once the attacker has established access, the compromised system may communicate with infrastructure controlled by the attacker.

This is called **Command and Control (C2)**.

The compromised system can effectively:

```text
Compromised System
        ↓
   "Phone Home"
        ↓
Attacker Infrastructure
        ↓
Commands
        ↓
Compromised System
```

The attacker can use this communication channel to control or interact with the compromised system.

### Connection to My Previous Learning

This connects with **Day 58 — Beaconing**.

Beaconing helped me understand the idea of a system repeatedly communicating with another destination.

In a malicious scenario, this type of communication can be a sign that an infected system is maintaining contact with attacker infrastructure.

### Defender Perspective

SOC analysts can monitor network activity for suspicious C2 behaviour.

This can include looking for:

- Repeated outbound connections
- Suspicious destinations
- Unusual communication patterns
- Beacon-like behaviour

---

# 11. Stage 7 — Actions on Objectives

## What is it?

This is the final stage of the Kill Chain.

At this point, the attacker performs the actions they originally wanted to accomplish.

Examples include:

- Stealing data
- Encrypting files for ransom
- Spying
- Sabotaging systems
- Moving deeper into the environment

The exact objective depends on the attack.

The overall flow is:

```text
Get In
  ↓
Stay In
  ↓
Communicate
  ↓
Achieve Objective
```

### Connection to the CIA Triad

This connects strongly to **Day 66 — The CIA Triad**.

An attacker's objective may affect:

- **Confidentiality** → stealing sensitive information
- **Integrity** → modifying or sabotaging data
- **Availability** → disrupting systems or encrypting files

This helped me see how the CIA Triad describes **what the attacker may ultimately impact**, while the Kill Chain describes **how the attacker moves through the attack process**.

# 12. Breaking the Chain

One of the most important lessons I took from this room is that defenders do not necessarily have to wait until the final stage.

The attack can potentially be disrupted at different points.

For example:

```text
Reconnaissance
      ↓
[Detect scanning]

Weaponization
      ↓
[Detect malicious tools/files]

Delivery
      ↓
[Block phishing]

Exploitation
      ↓
[Patch vulnerability]

Installation
      ↓
[Detect persistence]

C2
      ↓
[Block suspicious communication]

Actions on Objectives
      ↓
[Detect data theft / mass encryption]
```

The lesson's key defensive idea is:

> **The chain can be broken at different stages.**

This changed how I think about detection.

Instead of asking only:

> "Has the system already been compromised?"

I can also ask:

> "Which stage of the attack are we seeing?"

# 13. Defence in Depth

This connects directly to **Day 64 — Hardening Linux** and the idea of **defence in depth**.

Defence in depth means using multiple security layers rather than depending on one control.

For example:

```text
Email Filtering
      ↓
Patch Management
      ↓
Endpoint Detection
      ↓
Persistence Monitoring
      ↓
Network Monitoring
      ↓
Data Monitoring
```

If one security layer fails, another layer can still provide an opportunity to detect or stop the attack.

The Kill Chain makes this concept easier for me to understand because different controls can protect different stages.

---

# 14. Why Earlier Detection Matters

The lesson also showed me why detecting an attack earlier can be valuable.

Stopping a malicious email before it reaches a user is different from discovering the attack after an attacker has already established C2 communication.

A simplified progression is:

```text
Recon
  ↓
Delivery
  ↓
Exploitation
  ↓
Installation
  ↓
C2
  ↓
Data Theft / Destruction
```

As the attacker moves further through the chain, there may be more consequences to investigate and contain.

Therefore, earlier detection can provide an opportunity to disrupt the attack before the attacker reaches later objectives.

However, later-stage detections are still valuable because they can provide another chance to respond.

# 15. Kill Chain as a Detection Tool

As a SOC analyst, I can use the Kill Chain as a way of organising what I see in security telemetry.

| Kill Chain Stage | What I Might Look For |
|---|---|
| Reconnaissance | Scanning, probing, discovery activity |
| Weaponization | Malicious files, scripts, or prepared payloads |
| Delivery | Phishing emails, malicious links, attachments |
| Exploitation | Exploit activity, unexpected code execution |
| Installation | Suspicious persistence or malware installation |
| C2 | Beaconing and suspicious outbound communication |
| Actions on Objectives | Data theft, mass encryption, sabotage |

This gives me a structured way to think about alerts.

Instead of seeing an alert as an isolated event, I can ask:

```text
What happened?
      ↓
Which Kill Chain stage does it represent?
      ↓
What could have happened before it?
      ↓
What could happen next?
      ↓
What should I investigate?
```

# 16. Kill Chain in Incident Response

The Kill Chain can also help during incident response.

For example, if I detect suspicious C2 communication, I should not treat it as only a network event.

I can think:

```text
C2 detected
    ↓
How did the system get compromised?
    ↓
Was there phishing?
    ↓
Was a vulnerability exploited?
    ↓
Was persistence established?
    ↓
What commands were executed?
    ↓
What was the attacker trying to accomplish?
```

This gives me a more complete investigation path.

The stage also gives context about the seriousness and urgency of the situation.

For example:

- A blocked phishing attempt may require investigation but may have been stopped early.
- Active C2 communication can indicate that a compromised system is communicating with attacker infrastructure.
- Evidence of data theft can indicate that the attacker has reached an objective.
