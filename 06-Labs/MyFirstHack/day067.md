## 1. Introduction

Today I learned three words that are used very often in cybersecurity:

- **Threat**
- **Vulnerability**
- **Risk**

Before this lesson, these words could easily sound like they meant almost the
same thing. In cybersecurity, however, they have different meanings.

Understanding the difference is important because security professionals use
these terms when deciding:

- What could harm a system
- Where the weaknesses are
- How serious the situation is
- What should be fixed first
- What action should be taken

The main idea I learned today is:

> **Threat = What could harm me**  
> **Vulnerability = The weakness that could be exploited**  
> **Risk = The potential harm when a threat exploits a vulnerability**

---

## 2. Threat

A **threat** is a potential cause of harm.

It can be a person, activity, event, or situation that could cause damage to
confidentiality, integrity, or availability.

Examples include:

- Attackers
- Cybercriminals
- Nation-state actors
- Insiders
- Fire
- Flood
- Hardware failure
- Power outage
- Careless employees

A threat exists independently of my system. Attackers or disasters can exist
whether or not my system has a particular weakness.

```text
              THREAT
                 |
                 v
        Potential source
             of harm
                 |
        +--------+--------+
        |                 |
     Human              Non-human
        |                 |
    Attacker            Fire
    Insider             Flood
    Criminal            Failure
```

The main question I associate with a threat is:

> **"What could harm the system?"**

---

## 3. Vulnerability

A **vulnerability** is a weakness that a threat could exploit.

It can exist in:

- Software
- Hardware
- Systems
- Configurations
- Processes
- People

Examples include:

- An unpatched software vulnerability
- A weak password
- An unnecessarily open port
- Misconfigured permissions
- An employee who has not been trained to recognise phishing
- A building without proper fire protection

The important difference I learned is that a vulnerability is something
about **my system or organisation**.

Unlike threats, vulnerabilities are largely within my control.

For example, I can:

- Patch vulnerable software
- Strengthen weak passwords
- Close unnecessary ports
- Fix incorrect permissions
- Train employees

This is why hardening is important.

```text
           VULNERABILITY
                  |
                  v
               Weakness
                  |
       +----------+----------+
       |          |          |
    Software   Config     People
       |          |          |
    Unpatched   Open      Untrained
                Port       User
```

The main question I associate with a vulnerability is:

> **"What weakness could be exploited?"**

---

## 4. Threat vs Vulnerability

The easiest way I understand the difference is:

> **A threat is what could harm me.**  
> **A vulnerability is the weakness that lets it happen.**

| Threat | Vulnerability |
|---|---|
| Attacker | Weak password |
| Malware | Unpatched software |
| Insider | Excessive permissions |
| Fire | No fire suppression |
| Power failure | No backup power |

For example:

```text
Attacker
   |
   | exploits
   v
Weak Password
   |
   v
Unauthorised Access
```

The attacker is the **threat**.

The weak password is the **vulnerability**.

## 5. When Threat Meets Vulnerability

One of the most important concepts I learned today is that harm happens
when a threat meets a vulnerability.

A threat without a matching vulnerability may not be able to cause harm.

A vulnerability without a threat capable of exploiting it may also remain
unused.

```text
        THREAT
           |
           | exploits
           v
    VULNERABILITY
           |
           v
        HARM
```

This also explains why hardening works.

I cannot remove every attacker or disaster from the world, but I can reduce
the weaknesses they could exploit.

```text
Threat exists
     |
     v
Vulnerability removed
     |
     v
Threat has less opportunity
to cause harm
```

## 6. Risk

Risk is the potential for loss or harm when a threat exploits a
vulnerability.

It is not the threat by itself and it is not the vulnerability by itself.

Risk is the combination considered together with:

- **Likelihood**
- **Impact**

A simple way I understand it is:

```text
Threat
   +
Vulnerability
   |
   v
Likelihood + Impact
   |
   v
Risk
```

### Likelihood

Likelihood means:

> **How probable is it that the threat will actually exploit the vulnerability?**

This can depend on:

- How exposed the vulnerability is
- The capability of the threat
- The motivation of the threat
- How easily the weakness can be reached

### Impact

Impact means:

> **How much damage would happen if the event occurred?**

The impact can involve:

- Confidentiality
- Integrity
- Availability
- Money
- Reputation
- Safety
- Operations

---

## 7. Likelihood and Impact

I can think about risk using two basic questions:

```text
1. How likely is it to happen?

2. How bad would it be if it happened?
```

A situation that is both likely and damaging represents a higher risk.

A situation that is unlikely or has very little impact can represent a lower
risk.

```text
             RISK
              |
       +------+------+
       |             |
       v             v
  LIKELIHOOD       IMPACT
       |             |
       v             v
 How likely?      How bad?
```

This helped me understand why not every vulnerability deserves the same
priority.

---

## 8. Example: Weak Password

The lesson used a weak password as a practical example.

### Vulnerability

A system has a **weak password**.

### Threat

Attackers who attempt to guess passwords.

### Likelihood

The likelihood can be high because automated password guessing can happen
constantly on exposed systems.

### Impact

The impact depends on what the account can access.

If it is an administrator account on a critical system, the impact could be
very serious.

If it is a throwaway account on an isolated test machine with nothing
valuable, the impact could be much smaller.

```text
              WEAK PASSWORD
              Vulnerability
                    |
                    +
                    |
              PASSWORD GUESSING
                  Threat
                    |
                    v
            Likelihood + Impact
                    |
                    v
                   RISK
```

This example showed me that **the same vulnerability can have different
levels of risk depending on the situation**.

---

## 9. Why Risk Drives Security Decisions

Organisations cannot fix everything at the same time.

There are limited:

- Time
- Money
- People
- Resources

Therefore, security teams need to prioritise.

Risk helps them decide what needs attention first.

```text
High likelihood
       +
High impact
       |
       v
    HIGH RISK
       |
       v
   Fix urgently
```

A vulnerability that is difficult to exploit and would cause very little
damage may be a lower priority.

This is why simply saying:

> "This system is vulnerable."

does not tell the whole story.

I also need to understand:

> **What is the threat?**  
> **How likely is exploitation?**  
> **What would the impact be?**

That is risk-based thinking.

## 10. What Can Be Done About Risk?

Once a risk is identified, there are four fundamental ways of responding
to it:

1. **Reduce**
2. **Accept**
3. **Transfer**
4. **Avoid**

```text
                  RISK
                    |
       +------------+------------+
       |            |            |
       v            v            v
    Reduce       Accept      Transfer
                                  |
                                  v
                                Avoid
```

---

## 10.1 Reduce the Risk

This is the most common response.

I can reduce risk by:

- Patching software
- Adding security controls
- Hardening systems
- Improving configurations
- Reducing exposure

The goal is to lower the likelihood or impact.

This connects directly to **Day 64 — Hardening Linux**.

I cannot normally eliminate every threat, but I can reduce the weaknesses
that threats could exploit.

---

## 10.2 Accept the Risk

Sometimes an organisation consciously decides to accept a risk.

This does not mean ignoring the risk.

It means the risk has been assessed and is considered small enough to live
with, especially when fixing it would cost more than the potential harm.

The important word here is:

> **Consciously**

There is a big difference between:

```text
"I know about the risk and accept it."

vs.

"I never noticed the risk."
```

The first is a security decision.

The second is negligence.

---

## 10.3 Transfer the Risk

Risk can also be transferred to another party.

A common example is **cyber insurance**.

Insurance does not prevent the attack, but it can transfer some of the
financial impact to the insurer.

```text
Attack
  |
  v
Financial Loss
  |
  v
Insurance
  |
  v
Part of the impact transferred
```

The underlying security problem may still exist, but part of its impact is
shifted elsewhere.

---

## 10.4 Avoid the Risk

The final option is to avoid the risk completely.

This means not doing the risky activity or not using the risky system or
feature.

If a feature creates a risk that is not worth taking, an organisation may
decide not to build or use it.

This also connects with the idea of **attack surface**. If something is
removed completely, the risks associated with that component can also be
removed.

```text
Risky Feature
     |
     v
Don't use / remove it
     |
     v
Risk eliminated
```

---

## 11. The Four Risk Responses

| Response | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Reduce** | Lower likelihood or impact | Patch a vulnerability |
| **Accept** | Consciously live with the risk | Accept a low-impact risk |
| **Transfer** | Shift part of the impact | Cyber insurance |
| **Avoid** | Remove the risky activity | Do not use the risky feature |

These four options give me a basic understanding of how organisations
handle risk.

## 13. Thinking Like a Security Professional

The biggest lesson for me is that these terms are not just vocabulary to
memorise.

They give me a way to analyse security situations.

When I see a security problem, I can break it down into:

### Step 1 — Identify the Threat

> Who or what could cause harm?

### Step 2 — Identify the Vulnerability

> What weakness could be exploited?

### Step 3 — Determine the Risk

> How likely is exploitation and how serious would the impact be?

### Step 4 — Decide What to Do

> Should I reduce, accept, transfer, or avoid the risk?

```text
Threat
  |
  v
Vulnerability
  |
  v
Likelihood + Impact
  |
  v
Risk
  |
  v
Response
  |
  +--> Reduce
  +--> Accept
  +--> Transfer
  +--> Avoid
```

This turns security from a vague idea of:

> "Something bad might happen."

into a structured problem that can actually be analysed.

## 15. Why Precision Matters

Before this lesson, I could easily use phrases such as:

> "There is a security risk because the system is vulnerable."

Now I understand that these words should be separated.

For example:

> **Threat:** An attacker attempting to gain access.

> **Vulnerability:** A weak password on the exposed account.

> **Risk:** The possibility of unauthorised access, considering the
> likelihood of password guessing and the impact if the account is
> compromised.

This is much more precise.

I can see why security teams need this vocabulary when deciding what to fix
and what to prioritise.

The biggest thing I learned today is:

> **Threats are not the same as vulnerabilities, and vulnerabilities are
